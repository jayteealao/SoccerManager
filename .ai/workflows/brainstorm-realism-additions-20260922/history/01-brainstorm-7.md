# Brainstorm: realism additions to players, teams, tactics, and the match engine

## The Brainstorm

You started from one thought. The in-progress plans for Touchline build a lawful, tactical, calibrated match, and you wanted to know what they still miss on realism. Five sessions later, the answer is a whole product. Touchline is a football-management game, and the match engine is its first component. The engine workflow keeps its charter and its sixteen slices. The realism work runs as a separate programme after it, measured against the shipped engine with a performance budget of its own.

What we believe now, area by area:

- **The player.** A player becomes a person. The single position code becomes a competence rating per position, and each foot gets its own rating. Players gain new performance attributes, a personality with hidden traits, and habits that training can change. Under pressure or low mood, a strong habit can override an instruction, and the manager can see why.
- **The club.** A club has a style built from its identity, its squad, and its tactic. It also has a home ground, a reputation, and a team mood that personality shapes.
- **Tactics.** Each team has an in-possession shape and an out-of-possession shape, set-piece routines, and live changes that wait for the next stoppage. Both managers can make those changes.
- **The match.** The engine resolves every kind of action from the situation first and the player second. It uses the height the ball already has for play in the air. It has a full referee with a video assistant, and injuries have a severity. The player's own match plays in real time or as key moments, and the other matches of the day run beside it.
- **The realism test.** The test measures against real match data, and its bands are far tighter than the plan's first guess. Where the game departs from real data on purpose, the band has a real target and a design target. People watch matches and seasons at milestones to review them.
- **The season.** The season layer mirrors the real European structure. It adds careers with age curves, a market that values players from visible data only, and a club business with a board and an owner who can sack the manager.

The work runs in order. Engine realism comes first, starting with the test harness and then team shape and the block. The event-contract redesign comes next, and the season layer comes last. All twelve pieces are now written as three design documents.

Much is still open. Four areas are mapped but still empty: the staff around the squad, contracts and the dressing room, supporters and the press, and international football. The three design documents left 42 open questions, and this board answers some of them. The sharpest open tension is the licences. The two largest data sources for the test, StatsBomb's open data and the Transfermarkt set, may not allow a commercial game to use them for calibration. There is also a practical risk: the findings documents that every piece of work reads exist only in a gitignored folder on one machine.

## Decisions

### The in-progress plans and what they miss

- Every realism band is an output that the mechanisms must produce, never a value the engine is tuned to hit directly. The watched-match reviews catch what the bands miss (a review after each new mechanism, where the reviewer compares a simulated match with a real match replayed in the same 2D view, works through a checklist of named qualities, and judges openly what looks wrong), and a failed review reopens the question of whether more detail in the planned model produces football that reads as real. Why: Asked how to settle whether more detail produces real-looking football, the person chose a principle plus the watched review rather than waiting for a proof.
- The person's thought is three readings at once: the planned model needs more detail inside it, the numbers can pass while the football is still missing, and the realism test is too weak to catch either. Why: Asked which reading of the realism gap was theirs, the person answered with a mix of readings rather than a single one.
- Every idea on this board belongs to a second workflow, a realism programme started separately from the match-engine workflow already in flight. Why: Form and season context need a season model that the engine workflow excludes, so the ideas are kept out of the build in flight.
- The distillation produces several candidates split by dependency, one per coherent piece, rather than one large candidate. Why: So the cheapest pieces can start first.
- The realism work splits into two programmes: engine realism (the player as a person, the club and squad, Tier 3 tactics, conditions and officials, the rebuilt realism test, and both managers as entities) and the season layer that earns form. The engine-facing pieces start while the season layer is still being shaped. Why: The season layer had grown into a full management game with a calendar, cups, a table, and season-long availability, larger than the engine it was meant to follow, so one second workflow no longer fitted.
- Touchline is one product, a football-management game, and the match engine is its first component; the workflow in flight is stage one of a longer programme rather than the whole thing.
- The match-engine workflow in flight keeps its charter and its exclusions unchanged. The exclusions describe that workflow correctly even though they no longer describe the product, and splitting the work into an engine-realism programme and a season-layer programme is what keeps both statements true. Why: The exclusions still describe that workflow correctly, and the two-programme split keeps both descriptions true, so the work in flight pays nothing.
- The engine-realism programme builds team shape and the block first, following the measured law that the team slides with the ball and changes width by phase, so the first watched-match review judges the cheapest mechanism that changes how every match looks. Why: It is the cheapest mechanism that changes how every match looks, so the first review judges something visible early.
- The season-layer piece of work no longer stays one card: it splits into smaller cards that can each start on their own, and the split is written at the next distillation.
- Engine realism runs first and the season layer follows, as the decisions to put the realism work in a second workflow and to split it into two programmes already set it, so the four season cards (competitions, squad and careers, the market, and club business) build on a realistic match. Why: So the season cards build on a match that is already realistic.
- The card that asked when the player record changes shape becomes a recorded decision rather than a card, because the board already answered it: the record changes when the realism programme starts. The card to rebuild the realism test is rewritten at the next distillation to the test the board now holds: bands for per-match rates, scorelines and home advantage, pass completion, and physical output; a separate test-only output with per-player data; one internal match record behind both outputs; a watched-match review after each new mechanism with three comparisons; and bands plus a person's reading for player development and for club finances. Why: The player-record question was already answered, so it needs no investigation run, and the realism-test card no longer matched what the board had decided.

### The player as a person: attributes, personality, body, positions

- Where an attribute and a personality trait overlap, they split by scope: the attribute acts on each action in a match (composure on a shot under pressure, aggression on a tackle), and the trait acts on occasions and choices (a final, a penalty shoot-out, whether to dive or to foul cynically). Why: Otherwise the engine would read two values for one behaviour without saying what each one moves that the other does not.
- Four more traits join the eight already chosen, for twelve in all: versatility (willingness to play out of position), controversy (trouble off the pitch), money motivation (how much wages decide moves), and bravery (readiness for a 50-50, a header in traffic, or a block).
- The player record gains a fifth attribute group, personality, with its own slots outside the 50-attribute limit, so the twelve traits stop competing with new performance attributes for the 13 free slots. The record changes when the realism programme starts, as the decision on when the player record changes shape allows. Why: The twelve traits alone would take 12 of the 13 free slots and leave one slot for every new performance attribute.
- All four absent player fields matter: personality and traits; condition, morale, and form; age, height, and preferred foot; and positional familiarity.
- A player carries a competence rating for every position the player can fill, not one position code, so picking a lineup is a trade-off rather than slot-filling. Why: Picking a lineup should be a trade-off between players rather than filling slots.
- The gap between a strong player and a weak one is three things together: fewer errors, a higher ceiling of attempted actions, and more consistency. Why: Any one of the three on its own would read as a flat multiplier.
- A player carries a mood that the match itself moves, and that mood drives the player's decisions.
- Personality decides how strongly the player's own mood and the team's mood change that player's decisions; personality is the mediator, not a third mood.
- Clubs disagree about a player's worth for two reasons at once: each club sees a noisy estimate of the attributes, set by its scouting quality, and each club weighs the attributes differently by its playing identity and its manager's attributes. Why: If every club saw every attribute exactly, no club could value a player differently from another, and the market would have no disagreement to trade on.
- The player record changes shape when the realism programme starts and not before, following the precedent set for the event contract, which gains its player identifier only when the season-layer work starts. The match-rules and tactics-and-ai slices are therefore built against a record that will change under them. Why: It follows the same timing already chosen for the event contract change.
- Attribute effects are amplified on purpose beyond the sizes measured in real football, where skill moves pass, shot, and save outcomes only a few points, because a management game must reward a better player. The per-player residual band, which the realism test gained once it could separate the situation from the player, is expected to fail by design. Why: A management game must reward a better player, and an engine faithful to the measured sizes may make attributes feel as though they do not matter.
- A manager must see that a better player is better over a run of about five matches, through form and match ratings, rather than inside one match or only over a whole season.
- Consistency narrows the spread of every action, finishing and saving included, but by a different degree for each action. Why: Real finishing and saving barely repeat within a season, so a graded effect makes the departure from real data deliberate rather than accidental.
- Personality is hidden: the manager sees a description such as professional or volatile, whose accuracy is set by scouting quality and by time at the club, in the same way each club sees only a noisy scouting estimate of a player's attributes.
- A player carries at least four personality traits (professionalism, temperament, ambition and loyalty, and leadership), and more traits are wanted beyond these four.
- A player carries at least eight personality traits (professionalism, temperament, ambition and loyalty, leadership, determination, adaptability, sportsmanship, and pressure handling), each a hidden value from 1 to 100, and still more traits are wanted.
- Each foot carries its own rating, the strong foot is always 100, and two-footedness matters in play but must never become the dominant quality.
- The new performance attributes are marking, off-ball movement, set-piece skills (corners, free kicks, penalties, and long throws), flair, and consistency (the attribute that narrows the spread of every action by a different degree), which with 36 attributes today stays under the 50-attribute limit. Big-match nerve is left to the pressure-handling trait.
- Four limits keep two-footedness from dominating: a good weaker foot is rare and costs more in the market; the weaker foot costs something only when the ball is on that side or there is no time to shift it; the gap between the feet grows with pressure and distance; and defenders and AI managers show a one-footed player onto the weaker foot.
- A player's rating for a position rises with minutes played there and with training for it over months, so a manager can retrain a player for a new position.
- A player carries a few habits of play, such as cutting inside or shooting from distance, separate from attributes and personality, and training and coaching can add or remove a habit.
- Under pressure or low mood a strong habit can override the manager's instruction, and the manager can see why it happened. This amends the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given: it is carried out as given unless a strong habit overrides it at such a moment. Why: If a habit always yielded, habits would vanish under any instruction; if it always won, the manager's instruction would be weakened.
- A better player shows in four places: match ratings, statistics, visibly better choices and touches on the pitch, and staff reports whose accuracy depends on the staff's own quality.

### The club and the squad: identity, venue, mood, cohesion

- A team carries a mood of its own, separate from the mood of each player.
- Team mood is moved by match events, by the score and the clock together, by the crowd and the venue, and by the context before the match.
- Team mood is also moved by recent form, by the season so far, and by team context such as a club known for late goals or for slumps.
- Team mood shifts how players behave inside the chosen tactic and gates the error rate; it does not change the tactic itself.
- The club carries a home ground with a home advantage, a reputation and stature, a squad familiarity value for each tactic, and a playing identity.
- The squad has leadership, partnerships between pairs of players, and a cohesion value; cohesion is measured for the whole squad and again for the eleven on the pitch.
- A club's style has three layered sources: a club identity that survives a change of manager, the squad's own abilities, and the manager's tactic, and each adds its own part to how the club plays. Why: It produces the large club-to-club variation in style that the realism test must check and that nothing on the board produced before.
- The measured additive possession rule is an output, not an input: tactic, squad, and identity drive each match, one match may land far from the rule, and the season results must reproduce it. This amends the earlier reading that applied the rule as each match's default, so the default is now emergent rather than applied. Why: After a worked example, the person read the measured rule as the average: it was measured on real clubs whose managers already chose a tactic for every match, so adding a tactic on top of it would count that adaptation twice.
- Until the season layer exists, each test match sets form, table position, and the season situation by hand, so the mood mechanism and the AI manager can be tested in extreme states such as a relegation fight. Why: The season layer that would supply these values does not exist yet.

### Tactics beyond Tier 2: phases, transitions, set pieces, live changes

- The tactic has two phases, in possession and out of possession, each with its own shape and its own instructions, so the formation becomes two formations and the switch between them needs timing.
- Set pieces are designed routines with assigned takers, and the opponent sets a defensive routine against them, so a set piece is a duel rather than a dice roll.
- Tier 3 is per-player instructions and opposition instructions together, plus manager-set triggers for pressing, the defensive line, and similar situations. The person added the triggers themselves; they were not among the options offered.
- The manager authors two phases and the engine derives which of the nine paired phases it is in from its own state, so the tactic interface stays at two while the model runs nine. Why: It keeps the tactic interface simple while the model still runs the nine phases that real data shows.
- A set-piece routine changes four things: who wins first contact, where the ball goes (near post, far post, or short), where players stand for the second ball, and how many stay back against the counter. Why: Real corner data shows a set piece is decided by first contact, so the routine's levers are sized around it.
- The manager sets two team transition choices, to counter-press or drop back on losing the ball and to counter-attack or keep the ball on winning it, and each player carries an own transition instruction as a Tier 3 per-player instruction, which defaults sensibly from the team choices until the manager changes it.
- The manager sees the nine derived phases after the match only: the post-match report shows time spent in each phase and how the team did in each, and the match view does not show the current phase.

### How the match plays: actions, choices, duels, the ball in the air, officials, injuries

- The referee is a full official, with a foul tolerance, a card threshold, how often the referee plays advantage, how consistently the referee applies that threshold, and a leaning towards the home side that the crowd can move. Why: Asked whether the referee is a character, the person took the fullest option: a full official with a home leaning.
- Pitch condition and weather both exist and they interact, so rain on a worn pitch is worse than either one alone. Why: Asked whether conditions exist, the person took the fullest option: pitch and weather, interacting.
- An injury carries a severity, and its risk comes from what happens in play (hard tackles, fatigue past a threshold, a collision) rather than from a flat chance per minute. Why: The person took the fullest option over binary injuries; driving risk from hard tackles also finally gives the aggression attribute a real effect.
- The aerial model supports flight arcs, heading duels, goalkeeper claims, and spin and swerve, so a player's technique and preferred foot change where the ball ends up. Why: The person took the fullest option, using the ball height the engine already carries and that nothing yet uses.
- The engine build's benchmark tripwire (10 percent CPU and 25 percent memory over the match-rules baseline) guards the engine build against its own baseline and stops there; the realism programme measures against the shipped engine and sets a performance budget of its own. Why: The realism programme should pick a budget it can live in rather than inherit one written for a different purpose.
- The duel is a first-class contested action with types for ground attacking, ground defending, aerial, loose ball, and tackle, resolved from both players' attributes, their mood, and the ball's height and speed. It was first framed as the single place where player quality becomes an outcome; the person later corrected that part, so the duel is one such place among many. Why: Asked whether the duel becomes a primitive, the person said yes, a first-class contested action; the data had shown the duel is the most common contested action and the engine modelled none.
- Every action family is a place where player quality becomes an outcome, and the duel is only one of them: passes, through balls, positioning, off-ball movement, dribbles, shooting, goalkeeper actions, blocking and shielding, tackles and duels, and carrying and progressing the ball each need their own resolution. Why: The person corrected the board for treating the duel as the single place quality shows, naming the rest of the game; the data had already shown the pass has a completion surface a duel model does not produce.
- The gap an attribute makes between players lives in four places at once: in the choice a player makes (the pass picked, the moment to shoot), in the duel, in the body (pace, strength, stamina), and in the repeat, so a strong player also has fewer bad days. Why: Having chosen to amplify attribute effects so a management game rewards a better player, the person placed the gap in all four places.
- When the realism programme changes the event contract, the contract copies a real provider schema in full rather than a core subset, so the engine's output can feed any tool that reads that schema. Why: The person accepted the true size of the contract change rather than a core subset, so the output could be read by existing tools for that schema.
- The event contract mixes two providers: it keeps every DFL event kind and attribute and adds what StatsBomb carries that DFL does not, such as the freeze frame of player positions at each shot; this amends the earlier choice to copy a single provider schema in full.
- The engine keeps one internal record of each match, with one definition per event kind, and both the test-only output and the event contract are written from that record, so they can differ in what they show but never in a fact. Why: The person asked for a worked example first, then chose one record with two views so the realism test can never pass a match whose reported events disagree with what it measured.
- Where DFL and StatsBomb define the same event kind differently, the DFL definition wins, and StatsBomb only adds what DFL lacks. Why: Providers count the same action very differently, so a merged contract must pick one definition for each shared kind.
- An in-match knock is handled by all three parties according to severity: the medical staff take a severe injury off at once; for a minor knock the player's personality decides whether to play on, and the manager sees the knock and can overrule at the next stoppage; playing on raises the chance the knock becomes an injury and lowers the player's output. Why: It answers the long-open question of whether a manager may keep a hurt player on the pitch, and the person confirmed this severity split as their reading.
- A severe injury is carried across seasons more gently than in real football: a long absence, a shorter dip in form, and no career ended by injury, with personality and the medical staff moving a single player's return around that average.
- Full match simulation covers every match in the leagues the player chose; the simulated past and every other league keep the fast result-only model used at save creation and for unchosen leagues, so the rule that every other match is a full simulation amends neither. Why: Running every match everywhere as a full simulation would cost minutes per season before any realism addition, so the person limited it to the active leagues.
- The referee's home leaning, the rate of injuries during a match, and the effect of weather and pitch wear are all sized from published data rather than set by design, so the seventh build step needs a research pass before its design targets exist. Why: The person chose data over design where data is missing.
- The game has a video assistant referee: some goals, penalties, and red cards are checked and overturned, with the delays real football has, and whether a competition uses it can depend on the competition.
- The event contract uses the game's own names and structure from the start, with a mapping to the DFL and StatsBomb schemas, so no licence can block it. This amends the earlier choices to copy a provider schema and to merge DFL with StatsBomb, so no outside tool reads the output directly, while the rule that DFL wins each definition conflict still decides each definition. Why: Writing the contract in the game's own words removes the licence risk.

### The realism test: bands, design targets, watched reviews

- The realism test adds shape measures to the three aggregate bands: pass completion, where shots come from, possession in each third of the pitch, and goals by phase of play. Why: Three bands are not enough; with shape measures that the event stream already carries, a match that passes the bands and still looks wrong now fails something.
- Calibration groups runs by condition (dry, wet, worn) and sets a band for each, so rain is expected to lower goals rather than to fail the test. Why: Pitch wear and weather interact, so the bands either had to hold across every combination of conditions or calibration would fix the conditions and never test them; a band per condition lets a condition move the numbers without failing the test.
- The realism test reaches the season as well as the match: the shape of the league table, the shape of the market and of wages, and a watched season that a human reads and judges. Why: Transfers, wages, and budgets are the largest part of the product, and nothing tested whether a market, a wage bill, or a season of results reads as football; the person took every season measure offered.
- A human watches matches as part of the realism test, as a milestone review rather than as a gate on every build. Why: The argument that justifies a person watching a season also justifies watching a match, and a watched match is the first instrument that can check whether a fully detailed model actually reads as football.
- The realism test bands the spread as well as the average: how often a match finishes goalless, how often it finishes five-one, and how far a team's worst performance sits from its best. Why: Weak players swinging more and mood moving decisions both widen match-to-match variance, so the test has to measure the spread those mechanisms actually add.
- Mood, personality, and player quality change a team's scoring rate within a match and never the spread around it, so goals stay Poisson given that rate; the rule that a strong player differs from a weak one by fewer errors, a higher ceiling, and more consistency, and the rule that personality decides how strongly mood changes decisions, are both mechanisms for the rate rather than for the variance. Why: Real football's goal distribution is already Poisson, so the observed variance is fully explained by the scoring rate; extra variance would move the engine away from real football rather than towards it.
- The realism test bands four things: the measured per-match rates across five leagues, the scoreline distribution and home advantage, pass completion as a curve by kind of pass and by pass length, and the physical output of players (distance covered, time at each speed, and peak sprint speed). Why: The planned three bands are several times wider than the real spread between leagues, so they would pass an engine that is visibly wrong; the person took all four families.
- The best player's season totals, such as the top scorer's goals and the most assists, stay inside the range real leagues produce, so the season realism test bands them. Why: It caps the deliberate amplification of player effects from the season side.
- The realism test reads a separate, test-only output in which the engine writes per-player data; the event contract that the game reads stays as planned, and the rule that the contract gains its player identifier only when the season-layer work starts still holds. Why: The chosen bands need per-player data that the planned event contract does not carry, and a separate output lets the test run against the engine that ships without bringing the contract change forward.
- The realism bands measured with StatsBomb definitions are converted to DFL definitions, one mapping per shared event kind, and the 7 public DFL matches check each conversion. Why: DFL definitions win for every shared event kind, but the public DFL set is only 7 matches, too few to measure most bands on its own; converting keeps the large StatsBomb sample of 200 matches.
- The first watched-match review runs as soon as the first new mechanism runs, for example the choice model for when to shoot and which pass to pick, and a review follows each mechanism after it, so a failed mechanism is caught before other work builds on it. Why: A review run by hand only helps if it catches a failed mechanism before the rest of the model is built on it.
- The watched-match reviewer uses all three comparisons: a simulated match side by side with a real match replayed in the same 2D view, a checklist of named qualities (shape, tempo, decisions, set pieces), and open judgement of what looks wrong.
- The realism test checks player development two ways: it bands what the research measured (peak age by position, speed of decline, conversion of youth players, and the after-effects of injury), and a person reads a set of simulated careers for what no band covers. Why: The person asked for the real data first, then chose to band what was measured and add watched careers, the same pattern as the season test.
- The realism test checks finances two ways: it bands what the research measured (the revenue mix, the spread of broadcast income, league wage ratios, losses, transfer flows, and prize money), and a person reads a set of simulated club accounts for what no band covers. Why: The person asked for the real data first, then chose to band what was measured and add watched accounts, the same pattern as the career test.
- The career test's injury band is re-sized on purpose to a softer target, and the game is banded against that target rather than against the real after-effects of a severe knee injury (a mean 216 days out, two seasons below par, and 20 percent of professional careers ended within three seasons). Why: The game deliberately carries severe injuries more gently than real football, with a shorter dip in form and no career ended by injury, so a band on real after-effects would fail by design.
- Every band that departs from real football on purpose carries two numbers, the real target and a design target; the test passes on the design target and reports the distance from real data beside it, and this applies alike to the per-player band broken by the deliberate amplification of player effects, to the band for how consistency narrows each action, and to the softer injury band. Why: Otherwise a reader of the test would see one deliberate departure as a red failure and another as a green pass, with nothing to say that both are choices.
- Only build steps that change play get a watched review, so the first review follows team shape and the defensive block, and the test harness and the player record are checked by numbers alone; this answers the ninth open question of the engine realism design document.
- The design target for the deliberate amplification of player effects is that a top player looks clearly better than an average player in the same role in about two runs of five matches out of three, close to real football, so a manager needs more than one run to judge; this sets the size for the rule that a manager sees a better player over a run of about five matches. Why: It stays close to real football while still letting a manager see quality over a few runs.
- The usual top scorer and top assister stay inside the real range, and a record season beyond it is possible about as rarely as real record seasons happen; this sets how strict the limit on the best player's season totals is, and the real range was still unmeasured when this was chosen.

### The manager as a modelled entity

- The manager is an entity with attributes of their own, and those attributes affect the team's form and mood.
- Both managers carry attributes, and a manager weak at tactics has their instructions land weaker on the pitch, so the human player's own competence becomes part of the simulation. This mechanism was later replaced by the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given, with a weak tactician making poor choices instead. Why: The person wanted both managers to carry attributes that matter, so that the player's own competence is part of the simulation.
- An AI manager picks each match tactic from four inputs: its own philosophy, the opponent's strength and style, the match state by score and clock, and the season situation such as table position, a cup match, or a tired squad.
- No instruction lands weaker because of the manager; a manager weak at tactics makes poor tactical choices and understands the match poorly instead. This replaces the earlier mechanism in which a weak manager's instructions landed weaker, and the instruction a manager gives is always carried out as given. Why: The person corrected the earlier mechanism: there are no weak instructions, only poor choices, because a player told that their own instructions land weaker would read it as the game ignoring them rather than as realism.
- The human manager's tactical attribute acts on three things and never on an instruction: how accurate the manager's information is (opposition reports, match analysis, and scouting noise, the same noisy estimate of attributes a club's scouting produces), how fast the squad learns a new tactic (the squad's familiarity value for each tactic), and how the squad's mood and trust respond to the manager's choices over time. Why: The human player makes the human manager's choices, so once a weak tactician meant poor choices, the human's tactical attribute had nothing left to act on; the person chose these three things for it.
- A squad that distrusts a weak manager makes more errors after the same instruction, and that is mood rather than a weakened instruction: the instruction is carried out as given and the players carry it out worse, so the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given stays true. Why: The person judged it acceptable because the extra errors come from mood, not from the instruction itself.

### The season layer: competitions, careers, market, club business

- A season layer comes into scope: fixtures, results, and form are modelled properly, so this board describes a product larger than the match engine. Why: Form and season context cannot be supplied as asserted numbers in a team file; they need a season model behind them, so they are earned rather than asserted.
- The season layer contains fixtures and results, form, a league table with standings, season-long squad availability with suspensions, injury persistence, and carried fitness, and a full club calendar with cups, fixture congestion, and rotation.
- Form is computed at two scopes, a team form and a per-player form, and the per-player form feeds the player's mood, the mood that the match itself moves and that drives the player's decisions.
- A new save generates a synthetic backstory of past seasons, so partnerships, form, and club reputation start at believable values in the first match. Why: Partnerships between players cannot be generated for a squad without a shared history to derive them from, so the season layer supplies that history.
- The event contract gains its player identifier when the work to build the season layer starts and not before; the engine ships on the contract it has, and the realism programme pays the migration across every part of the build that reads the event stream by then. Why: The stream-protocol part of the engine build was already complete with other parts depending on it, and changing the contract later was judged cheaper than disturbing a complete piece of work now.
- The generated backstory is one past season deep: a form record, a final table position, and partnerships earned from minutes played together. This was later replaced by several fully simulated past seasons.
- The season layer includes transfers and finances, with a market, budgets, and wages, so the product is a full football-management game rather than a match engine with a season around it.
- A player's ability changes over seasons along age curves, one per attribute, and the curves differ from player to player with the variance real players show in how they develop. Many other factors move the curves, and which factors they are is still open.
- The manager runs a training plan between matches, setting focus and intensity, and training moves fitness, injury risk, the squad's familiarity with each tactic, and player development.
- Five factors move a player's age curves: playing time and the level played at, training and coaching, injuries, personality, and moments. The list stays open, so other factors may be added later.
- Moments are of two kinds and both move a player's curve: match moments such as a debut, a decisive goal, or a costly error in a final, and career turning points such as a transfer, a loan, a new manager, or a first-team breakthrough.
- A new save simulates several past seasons in full at save creation, so every player's current ability, partnerships, and reputation agree with a simulated past. This replaces the earlier plan of a backstory only one past season deep. Why: One past season was judged not enough history for a partnership to read as earned.
- The past seasons simulated at save creation run a fast result-only model instead of the full match, so save creation stays short while the present runs the full engine. Why: Running every past match on the full engine would make save creation too slow; a single league season already takes minutes on one core.
- A loan's effect varies widely with the club, the coach, and the minutes, and it can help or hurt, while the average effect across loans stays near zero, as the Bundesliga loan study measures. Why: It keeps the only measured evidence on loans true while still letting a single loan matter to a player's career.
- Each club has a board and an owner type, for example patient, ambitious, or frugal, that sets targets for league position, cups, finances, and style, sets how much patience the board has, and can sack the manager; sackings apply to AI managers as well.
- A transfer fee is a shared market valuation plus a premium from each club's own view, and the season test bands the resulting fees against real fees and valuations by age and position from the public Transfermarkt career dataset.
- The shared market valuation comes only from what every club can see (age, minutes, level, goals, recent fees, and reputation), as a Transfermarkt value does, and it never reads the true attributes. Why: A valuation computed from the true attributes would tell every club the truth that scouting noise hides, and clubs would stop disagreeing about a player.
- A sacked human manager keeps the save: the manager is unemployed, can apply for jobs, and receives offers based on reputation.
- The player chooses at save creation which countries and divisions are active, so more leagues give more jobs and more clubs to deal with, at the cost of a slower season.
- A club has four income sources: matchday income from stadium size, prices, and attendance; broadcast and prize money by league position and cup progress; commercial income from reputation and success; and owner money that the club's owner type can put in or take out within limits.
- Every league the player did not choose runs the fast result-only model used for the simulated past, so its players play, develop, and gain value, and the whole world moves while only the chosen leagues run the full engine.
- Player-sale income enters the club's finances, and the club's owner type decides how much of it reaches the manager's budgets and how much pays debts or wages. Why: Real selling clubs earn a large share of their income from player sales, and with only four income sources a selling club in the game could not earn what a real one does.
- Leagues and competitions carry financial rules such as a squad-cost cap, and a breach brings a sanction: a fine, a transfer ban, or a points deduction.
- The work to build the season layer splits into four cards: competitions (table, cups, calendar, congestion, suspensions), squad and careers (form, availability, training, development, the simulated past), the market (scouting, valuation, fees, transfers, loans), and club business (board and owner, income, wages, financial rules, sacking, the job market). Why: The season layer had grown too large for one piece of work, and smaller cards can each start on their own.
- A later version of the game loads a real-life database whose players start at the current real season, possibly imported by the player, so the world must accept imported seasons; until then the player chooses how many past seasons a new save simulates.
- Leagues, cups, and competitions mirror the real European structure in full, starting with the leagues of every UEFA member association, with their divisions, promotion and relegation, domestic cups, and European qualification.
- AI clubs get the effect of training from their staff quality and their manager, with no day-by-day plan; only the player's club runs the detailed training plan the manager sets between matches.
- Manager sacking rates and board patience are sized from published data rather than set by design. Why: The person asked for it to be looked up, and then for every open data gap to be researched before the board continued.

- The minimum event fields that kloppy and floodlight agree on are the event type, the period and timestamp, the start coordinates and (where the event has an end) the end coordinates, the team identifier, the player identifier, and an outcome. Source: kloppy v3.19.0 kloppy/domain/models/event.py:686.
- The ball is in play for 59 percent of recorded frames, about 55 minutes, and its speed has a median of 5.9 metres per second, a ninetieth percentile of 15.1, and a maximum near 57 (2 Metrica matches, 25 frames per second). Source: .scratch/out/metrica2.json (2 matches, 25 fps tracking plus events).
- Most possession is brief: a match holds about 475 possession chains, 67.8 percent of them carry no pass or one pass, the median chain lasts 2.9 seconds, and only one chain in 20 ends in a shot (2 Metrica matches). Source: .scratch/out/metrica2.json (2 matches, 25 fps tracking plus events).
- A player on the ball has an opponent within 5 metres for 56 percent of touches, with the nearest opponent a median 4.4 metres away, and has on average only 1.16 teammates within 10 metres (3366 StatsBomb 360 freeze frames). Source: .scratch/data/sb360/f360.json (3366 freeze frames).
- High intensity concentrates into attacking sequences rather than spreading across the match: during goal-scoring sequences players average 3.16 metres per second and spend 8.8 percent of the time above 7 metres per second, against a match average of 1.67 and 0.25 to 0.44 percent (19 goal sequences at 20 frames per second, Last Row data). Source: .scratch/data/lastrow (19 goal sequences at 20 fps).
- Across all passes, pressure appears not to matter, moving completion by only seven points and not in order, because the purpose of a pass is confounded with pressure; restricted to flat passes the curve is monotone and spans twenty points, from 79.8 percent with an opponent inside two metres to 100 percent beyond twelve (1650 passes joined to tracking within 9 milliseconds). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_sync.csv (1650 passes joined to tracking at 9 ms).
- The duel is the second most common on-ball event at 201.7 a match in DFL data, which reconciles the earlier figure of 456 a match because Wyscout logs one row per participant while DFL logs one per contest; a duel turns the ball over in only 29.4 percent of cases (7 DFL matches, 9961 events). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- The ground duel is sharply asymmetric: when the ball carrier wins, possession changes hands 6.4 percent of the time, and when the defender wins it changes 51.4 percent, while the aerial duel is nearly symmetric at 31.2 against 36.6 percent, so a model with one win probability and an automatic turnover would roughly double real turnover rates (7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- Fouls are an outcome of the duel rather than a separate mechanism: 10.6 percent of duels resolve as a foul, giving 21.4 a match against the 22.9 foul events the schema records, so an engine that models duels obtains fouls, free kicks, and cautions without a separate foul generator (7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- The duel looked dominant partly because the DFL schema files several action families inside one element: take-ons at 14.6 a match with 65.7 percent success, shielding as retained control at 17.6, and beaten defenders at 7.3 all live inside the TacklingGame element, so its 201.7 contests a match are a container rather than a single action (7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- Evidence now covers the pass, through ball, cross, shot, dribble, duel, shot block, positioning, and aggregate off-ball movement, while three families have no outcome evidence at all: the carry, which no schema read records as an event; the ten off-ball run types that SkillCorner names but nothing has counted; and shielding outside a recorded duel. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- The full action set is 53 actions, 38 outfield and 15 goalkeeper; public data measures 39 in full and 10 partially, and feints only thinly, and nothing public measures marking and cover assignments, keeper organisation, or keeper dive movement. Source: .scratch/out/ACTIONS.md.
- The carry is measured on three providers: 273 to 287 carries of at least 5 metres a match (corrected from an earlier 271), at about 4 metres per second, with 79.9 percent kept, longer carries faster and riskier, and centre-backs carrying the most because they receive with the most space (7 DFL matches among the sources). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_control_segments.csv and idsse_gk_positions.csv.gz (7 DFL matches).
- Pressure at the moment of receiving is the common input across actions: from no pressure to very high pressure, possession loss rises from 1.0 to 32.6 percent, pass completion falls from 93.4 to 67.0 percent, and one-touch play rises from 5.6 to 31.3 percent; DFL tracking gives the same gradient from geometry alone, 93.4 percent kept with no opponent inside 10 metres against 60.9 with one inside 1 metre (20 SkillCorner matches). Source: .scratch/out/sc_dynamic_events.csv.gz (20 SkillCorner matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- Off-ball runs happen 495 times a match across ten subtypes, and a run's danger and its completion move in opposite directions: safe runs such as coming short and dropping off are received 91 to 98 percent of the time when targeted and almost never lead to a shot, while runs in behind and runs to receive a cross are received 40 to 52 percent of the time and lead to a shot 20 to 29 percent of the time (20 SkillCorner matches). Source: .scratch/out/sc_dynamic_events.csv.gz (20 SkillCorner matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- A single pressing engagement is credited with a regain only 18.5 percent of the time, yet 80.6 percent of pressing chains end in a regain, and pressing in the opponent's third regains 32.6 percent against 25.4 in midfield, so the value of pressing is collective (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/sb_events.csv.gz (200 StatsBomb matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- Bodies between the ball and the goal decide shots: with no defender in the shot cone a shot scores 12.4 percent of the time and is blocked 20.9 percent, with two defenders it scores 5.4 and is blocked 40.4, and a shooter has an opponent inside 5 metres 88.2 percent of the time (4671 non-penalty shots with StatsBomb 360 frames). Source: .scratch/out/sb360_actions.csv (4671 non-penalty shots with 360 frames).
- A keeper's save rate falls with shot quality, from 89.1 percent below 0.05 expected goals to 27.2 percent above 0.4; two saves in three are not held (a fifth go behind and a fifth are parried into danger), and 87 percent of goals are conceded without the keeper touching the ball (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/sb_events.csv.gz (200 StatsBomb matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- Keeper depth follows a measured rule: out of possession with the ball in the keeper's own half, depth is about 0.23 metres per metre of ball distance from goal, the keeper spends 42 percent of live time beyond the depth of the box, shifts 0.17 metres sideways per metre the ball moves across, and stands a median 2.2 metres off the line at the moment of a shot, where depth has no effect on conversion once shot quality is known (7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_control_segments.csv and idsse_gk_positions.csv.gz (7 DFL matches).
- Keepers distribute 66 times a match between them at 73.5 percent completion, with short passes and throws above 97 percent and punts at 29.1; short goal kicks are 45.3 percent of goal kicks and complete at 99.4 percent against 44.1 for long ones, and the 2019 goal-kick law halved the median goal kick from 65.6 metres in 2017/18 to 31.3 metres in 2020 to 2024 (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/sb_events.csv.gz (200 StatsBomb matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- Dribbles happen 27.4 times a match at 53.0 percent success, success falls up the pitch from 61.5 percent in a team's own third to 48.2 in the final third, and role changes how often a player dribbles but not how often the dribble succeeds: wingers attempt six times as many as centre-backs, at about the same success rate as full-backs and forwards (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/sb_events.csv.gz (200 StatsBomb matches) and ACTIONS.md.
- Shielding is common but barely coded: event feeds record 1.3 shields a match, while tracking shows about 80 possessions a match held against an opponent inside 2 metres, 10 of them stationary, with about two in three kept (7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_control_segments.csv and idsse_gk_positions.csv.gz (7 DFL matches).
- The decision to shoot is a steep threshold at the edge of the box: 87.7 percent of on-ball releases inside 6 metres are shots, 72.1 at 6 to 11 metres, 35.3 at 11 to 16.5, 20.2 at 16.5 to 22, and 6.2 at 22 to 30, and pressure halves the shooting rate outside 11 metres; under pressure the share of releases that are passes falls from 85.5 to 52.9 percent as clearances, dribbles, and dispossessions appear (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_sb.txt (200 StatsBomb matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- Players choose the safe open option more often than the dangerous one: over 70,059 passes with freeze frames, a four-term score (space around the receiver, clearance of the passing lane, distance, and forward progress) picks the real receiver 55.5 percent of the time against 14.9 by chance, and among SkillCorner's tracked options the safest is chosen 45.0 percent of the time and the most threatening 31.7, below the 36.0 chance rate (200 StatsBomb matches and 20 SkillCorner matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_sb.txt (200 StatsBomb matches) and FINDINGS.md §16; verified .scratch/out/gap_sc.txt (20 SkillCorner matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- The match changes with the clock: minutes 75 to 90 produce goals 41 percent faster than minutes 0 to 15 and added time 70 percent faster, passing tempo falls from 10.3 to 8.2 passes a minute and to 7.0 in added time, and cautions rise fivefold (1826 Wyscout league matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- The score changes behaviour once team strength is removed: a team trailing by one goal scores at 1.10 times its own expected rate and a team leading by one at 0.92; the trailing team's possession rises above its expectation from 2 points early to 12 points after the 90th minute; the leading team takes its throw-ins, goal kicks, and free kicks at a median 22.0 seconds against 15.1 for the trailing team; and the team that scores first wins 69.4 percent of the time (1826 Wyscout matches, strength-adjusted, and 7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy_state.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches, strength-adjusted) and FINDINGS.md §16; verified .scratch/out/gap_idsse_an.txt (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- On the ground, local numbers decide duels: with all 1,412 DFL duels joined to tracking, the defender wins 42.7 percent when outnumbered within 5 metres, 44.2 when level, 53.2 with one extra teammate, and 66.7 with two, while in the air support makes no difference once the defender is not outnumbered (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events). Source: .scratch/out/gap_idsse_an.txt (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- Loose balls are a real phase: a clearance is next controlled by the clearing team only 26.5 percent of the time, the team of a player who wins a header controls the next ball only 48.0 percent of the time against 39.3 for the loser's team, and 6.2 percent of shots are rebounds within 5 seconds, which convert at 16.4 percent against 11.3 (200 StatsBomb matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_sb.txt (200 StatsBomb matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- The ball is dead for 42.2 minutes a match, and each restart has a measured stoppage: a throw-in takes a median 13.8 seconds, a goal kick 23.2, a corner 31.8, a free kick 32.5, and the kick-off after a goal 68.2 (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events). Source: .scratch/out/gap_idsse_an.txt (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- Referees favour the home side measurably: in Serie A 2007-08 matches played behind closed doors, home bias was estimated at 23 percent for fouls, 26 percent for yellow cards, and 70 percent for red cards; Champions League referees gave away teams 25 percent more yellow cards than home teams and Europa League referees 10 percent more, after controlling for dominance. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176510001497 and https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02640414.2017.1377911 (research sub-agent).
- The crowd drives the referee's leaning: in Bundesliga matches without spectators during COVID-19, the home team's foul advantage of about 0.6 a match reversed to about one foul against, home cards rose significantly, the bias scales with crowd size, and home advantage in results shrank but did not vanish. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165176520303815 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8011816/ (research sub-agent).
- Added time leans towards the home side: referees add about 44 seconds less when the home team leads by one goal than when the away team does, about 60 seconds less than an unbiased benchmark with fans present, and the gap disappears without a crowd (from a study summary only). Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2773161825000011 (summary only) (research sub-agent).
- A video assistant referee overturns a decision about once every three to four matches: 109 overturns in the 2019-20 Premier League season from about 2,400 checks, and 123 in 2020-21, split into 34 goals awarded, 42 disallowed, 29 penalties awarded, and 22 penalties overturned, with an average delay of about 50 seconds per review; LaLiga reviews about 12.55 incidents a match. Source: https://global.espn.com/football/story/_/id/37587139 and https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37501924 (research sub-agent).
- Elite match injury incidence is about 21 injuries per 1000 match hours in 2022-23, down from 27.5 in 2001 to 2008, which is roughly one match injury per team every two to three matches; hamstring injuries are 24 percent of all injuries and rising. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406901/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9985757/ (research sub-agent).
- Injury risk rises late in each half, with fixture congestion (5 of 8 studies find more match injuries with 4 days or fewer of rest, and muscle injuries rise within 5 days of the previous match), and with age, at an odds ratio of about 1.1 a year and 1.4 a year for hamstring strains. Source: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9758680/ and https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546503258912 (research sub-agent).
- Conditions are measured only in part: altitude moves the expected goal difference by about half a goal per 1000 metres of difference, heat cuts running distance sharply (about 26 percent at 43 degrees against 21 degrees, weakly sourced), artificial turf shifts the injury mix toward ankle sprains (4.83 against 2.66 per 1000 match hours) without raising elite injury totals, and no source quantifies rain, wind, or pitch wear. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18156225/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10139885/ (research sub-agent).

### The realism test: bands, design targets, watched reviews

- The realism test the engine plan already holds is three aggregate bands: goals per match from 2.4 to 3.2, shots per team from 8 to 16, and possession from 35 to 65 percent. Source: .ai/workflows/football-manager-match-engine/03-slice-calibration.md:33.
- Across 1826 matches in five top leagues in 2017/18, goals per match average 2.709 with a standard deviation of 1.675, and the distribution of total goals is Poisson to within 1.5 percentage points at every count, with a variance-to-mean ratio of 1.035. Source: .scratch/out/wyscout_rates.json (1826 matches, five leagues 2017/18).
- Home teams score 1.530 goals a match and away teams 1.179, a home advantage of 0.351 goals, and results split 45.3 percent home wins, 24.5 percent draws, and 30.2 percent away wins (1826 matches, five leagues, 2017/18). Source: .scratch/out/wyscout_rates.json (1826 matches, five leagues 2017/18).
- Per-match rates are tight across five different leagues: passes 839 to 888, pass accuracy 82.2 to 83.3 percent, shots 21.0 to 23.2, shot conversion 9.7 to 11.1 percent, duels 442 to 471, aerial duels 76 to 99, fouls 21 to 29, yellow cards 3.1 to 4.9, throw-ins 42 to 46, corners 9.5 to 10.3, goal kicks 16 to 17, free kicks 19 to 27, and offsides 4.0 to 5.0. Source: .scratch/out/wyscout_rates.json (1826 matches, five leagues 2017/18).
- Shot conversion halves roughly every 5 metres: 51.9 percent inside 6 metres, 21.6 percent at 6 to 11, 12.2 percent at 11 to 16.5, 6.3 percent at 16.5 to 22, 2.8 percent at 22 to 30, and 1.8 percent beyond 30, measured over 40461 shots. Source: .scratch/out/wyscout_rates.json (1826 matches, five leagues).
- In provider-labelled tracking over 14 halves, the ball sits above half a metre for 29.6 percent of live play, above one metre for 23.9 percent, and above two metres for 17.1 percent, and it is in play for 55.4 percent of the match, so a strictly two-dimensional engine is wrong about a sixth of the live game. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_ball.csv (14 ball halves, provider-labelled).
- Measured ball speed runs to a median of 6.47 metres per second, a ninety-ninth percentile of 24.9, and an extreme of 49.9, which confirms the earlier discard of a 57 metres per second reading as tracking noise and gives the engine a real speed ceiling in its place. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_ball.csv (14 ball halves, provider-labelled).
- The official DFL live schema carries 31 event kinds and 139 distinct attributes at 1423 events a match, against the engine's four kinds carrying no player, no coordinates, and no outcome; the earlier estimate that the contract change means about fifteen event kinds plus a player, coordinates, and an outcome understates the gap by half on kinds and by a factor of forty on fields (7 DFL matches, 9961 events). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- Whether a pass leaves the ground matters more than how far it travels: flat passes complete at 90.2 percent and high passes at 53.4, and a flat long pass completes at 68.6 percent while a high medium pass completes at 52.0, so the engine should branch on pass height before pass length. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- Only about a third of shots reach the goalkeeper, because 38.4 percent go wide and 22.6 percent are blocked by a defender first, so a shot model that resolves attacker against goalkeeper models the minority case. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_events.csv.gz (7 DFL matches, 9961 events).
- Data providers count the same action very differently: duels run at 64, 202, or 226 a match and through balls at 4.6, 15.6, or 44.1 depending on the provider, while providers that share a definition agree tightly, with carries of at least 5 metres at 287 against 273 (corrected from an earlier 271) and possession duration at 1.20 against 1.16 seconds; so every realism band must name the definition it copies. Source: .scratch/out/ACTIONS.md.
- The stream crate already serves a recorded fixture over the same protocol the viewer reads, at 50 ticks a second, so a real match converted into a fixture can play in the viewer beside a simulated one. Source: crates/stream/src/replay.rs:1 and crates/stream/src/replay.rs:14.
- The realism test has measured joint bands: goals are Poisson once the club is known, with a within-club variance-to-mean ratio of 0.99, but shots are over-dispersed at 1.64 and crosses at 3.03; one team's shots run from 6 to 17 between the 10th and 90th percentiles, shots and goals correlate at only 0.32, and home and away shot counts correlate at minus 0.34 (1826 Wyscout league matches). Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
- Over about 20 seasons the league top scorer's goals run from 19 to 36 in the Premier League, 21 to 36 in Serie A, 16 to 41 in the Bundesliga, and 15 to 38 in Ligue 1, with medians of about 26 to 29, and LaLiga holds the outlier at Messi's 50 in 2011-12; the Premier League assist record is 21, and no source gives the tenth-best scorer's total. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capocannoniere and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bundesliga_top_scorers_by_season and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_League_Golden_Boot (research sub-agent).
- The calibration sources carry different licences: the IDSSE DFL set and the Wyscout public set are CC-BY 4.0 and SkillCorner's open data is MIT, all usable commercially with attribution, while StatsBomb's open data is framed for research under a user agreement, Metrica's sample data has no formal licence, and the CC0 Transfermarkt dataset is scraped data that Transfermarkt's own terms and the EU database right may still cover. Source: https://github.com/spoho-datascience/idsse-data and https://figshare.com/collections/Soccer_match_event_dataset/4415000 and https://github.com/SkillCorner/opendata/blob/master/LICENSE and https://github.com/statsbomb/open-data (research sub-agent).

### The manager as a modelled entity

- Substitutions follow the score: across 1826 Wyscout league matches in 2017/18 a team makes 2.85 changes per match at median minutes 61, 72, and 82, a team that trails makes its first change at minute 57 against 66 for a team that leads, midfielders are half of the players taken off, and only 6.5 percent of changes happen at half time. Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.

### The season layer: competitions, careers, market, club business

- The board believed the match event carried only a team identifier, a score, and four event types (kick-off, goal, full-time, and tactics-change) and no player identifier. The code by the fifth session shows 17 event types and an optional player identifier, in the protocol crate's event definition. Source: crates/protocol/src/event.rs:16 and crates/protocol/src/event.rs:146 (17 event types and an optional player identifier, at session 5).
- Today's engine plays one match in 387 milliseconds of wall time on one core of an AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D, so one 20-club league season of 380 matches takes about 147 seconds on one core and about 20 seconds on eight, before any realism addition makes a match more expensive. The match time is measured by the engine benchmark; the season figures are arithmetic. Source: .ai/workflows/football-manager-match-engine/bench-baseline/viewer-pitch/bench-1.stdout.txt:6 (bench.match_wall_ms 387; the season figures are arithmetic).
- The largest public career dataset is derived from Transfermarkt: 50,000 players, 65 competitions, 1.89 million appearances with minutes, 650,000 market valuations, and 175,000 transfers, released under CC0. It measures playing time, level, moves, and market judgement, and it carries no ratings. Source: https://github.com/dcaribou/transfermarkt-datasets (research sub-agent).
- Performance peaks at about 27.4 across 15,591 top-five-league player-seasons, at 26 to 27 for wingers and full-backs and at 27.5 to 28 for centre-backs and strikers. Wingers fall to half their peak by 31 and centre-backs by 35.2, and an 18-year-old in a top league plays at 50 to 85 percent of the eventual peak. Source: https://macro-football.com/other/aging/ (research sub-agent).
- Physical output has its own curves: across 5,203 GPS match performances speed peaks at 25.7, endurance at 24.8, and explosiveness at 26.0, and high-intensity output falls after 32 while endurance holds. Creative passing holds into the early thirties while shooting and tackling fade. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12551122/ (abstract) and https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37467220/ (research sub-agent).
- Of about 200 LaLiga academy players aged 13 to 18, 4 percent reached the top tier within ten years. Across 378 Bundesliga loans, loanees gained no measurable minutes and no measurable market value compared with 6,162 players who were not loaned. Source: https://www.essex.ac.uk/news/2024/06/27/just-four-per-cent-of-teenage-academy-players-make-it-to-top-tier-football and https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/umiodp/295740.html (research sub-agent).
- After an ACL rupture 80 percent of elite players return, after a mean of 216 days; they stay below matched controls for two seasons, with attackers declining further, 20 percent of professionals see their careers end within three seasons, and only 47.5 percent keep their playing level. Source: https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671211008892 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859836/ (abstracts; research sub-agent).
- No public data records true attribute histories, training or coaching effects at scale, personality across a career, or the causal effect of a moment. The only attribute-style history is the FIFA 15 to 23 rating set, which is rated by people rather than measured. Source: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/stefanoleone992/fifa-23-complete-player-dataset (research sub-agent).
- The revenue mix is measured: the top 20 clubs earn 43 percent from commercial income, 38 percent from broadcast, and 19 percent from matchday. The top ten lean on commercial income at 48 percent while clubs ranked 11 to 20 lean on broadcast at 49 percent, and the Premier League as a whole takes about half of its 6.8 billion pounds from broadcast (Deloitte Football Money League and Annual Review). Source: https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/services/consulting-financial/analysis/deloitte-football-money-league.html and the Deloitte Annual Review pages (research sub-agent).
- In a selling league player sales are a main income source: across nine Eredivisie clubs in 2022/23 they were 29.5 percent of income on average and about 44 percent at Ajax and PSV. Source: https://offthepitch.com/a/player-sales-lifeline-dutch-football-financial-divide-widens (research sub-agent).
- Revenue spread inside a league is set largely by broadcast rules: Premier League central payments run 1.6 to 1 from bottom to top with about 2.7 million pounds of merit pay per place, Bundesliga TV money runs 2.7 to 1, two clubs take 52 percent of LaLiga revenue, and Eredivisie revenue runs about 10 to 1. Source: https://www.football365.com/news/2025-26-premier-league-prize-money-table-tv and https://bulinews.com/bundesliga-money-distribution-for-2025/26-season-how-much-club-get (research sub-agent).
- Wages run at 54 to 80 percent of revenue across the big-five leagues (Bundesliga 54, LaLiga 60, Premier League 65, Serie A 66, Ligue 1 80), and UEFA's squad-cost rule caps wages, transfer costs, and agent fees at 70 percent of revenue from 2025/26. Source: the Deloitte Annual Review pages and https://www.uefa.com/news-media/news/0274-14da0ce4535d-fa5b130ae9b6-1000--explainer-uefa-s-new-financial-sustainability-regulations/ (research sub-agent).
- Losses are normal and owners cover them: European top-division clubs lost 1.1 billion euros before tax in 2024, only 8 Premier League clubs made an operating profit, and Championship clubs lost 355 million pounds with one club in operating profit. Owner-funding totals are only a proxy built from club examples. Source: https://www.uefa.com/news-media/news/02a2-200452a66064-0cfd3f86b94f-1000--new-report-highlights-record-revenues-and-increasing-inv/ and https://theesk.org/2025/07/19/15922/ (research sub-agent).
- The richest league buys and the others sell: in summer 2025 Premier League clubs spent 3.0 billion pounds gross, about 44 percent of league revenue and 51 percent of all big-five gross spending, while the Bundesliga and Ligue 1 were net sellers by 180 and 305 million euros. Source: https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/about/press-room/deloitte-premier-league-spending-record-eclipsed.html (research sub-agent).
- Prize money is published in full: the Champions League pays 18.62 million euros for the league phase, 2.1 million per win, and rising round payments up to 18.5 million for the final, from a 2.467 billion euro pot, against 565 million for the Europa League. Source: https://www.beinsports.com/en-us/soccer/uefa-champions-league/articles/uefa-confirms-prize-distribution-for-2025-26-champions-league-2025-08-27 (research sub-agent).
- Managers are sacked often and sacking does not work: the median top-flight tenure is about 243 days, 47 English managers were dismissed in 2014-15 and 51 in 2025-26, and across 331 mid-season changes in the top two divisions of five countries from 2017-18 to 2021-22, points collapsed before a dismissal while expected points stayed stable, and the new manager's effect against matched controls was indistinguishable from zero. Source: https://football-observatory.com/Coaches-average-tenure-duration-global-analysis and https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2026.2698238 (research sub-agent).
- A sacking follows results against expectation rather than underlying performance: a run of defeats, the relegation zone, and results worse than the pre-season bookmaker expectation all raise the risk, and of 78 Premier League clubs that sacked a manager in the relegation zone, 58 percent went down anyway. No study compares sacking rates by owner type or measures how fast a sacked manager finds work. Source: https://doi.org/10.3390/economies8040082 and https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/13218236 (research sub-agent).
- The CIES transfer-value model estimates fees from performance, age, position, contract length, and club, and its estimates correlate with fees paid at 76 to 86 percent. In 34 of 49 cases studied the fee sat within 2 million euros of the estimate, with a long tail of star overpayment such as Mbappe at 87 million euros over. Source: https://football-observatory.com/Transfer-window-analysis-over-and-under-paid (research sub-agent).
- UEFA has 55 member associations, 53 of which place clubs in European competition, with places set by a five-year coefficient. The three club competitions use a 36-club league phase since 2024-25, and the official 2025-26 access list defines which domestic place enters which round. Source: https://kassiesa.net/uefa/data/method5/crank2026.html and https://documents.uefa.com/r/Regulations-of-the-UEFA-Champions-League-2025/26/Annex-A-Access-List-for-the-2025/26-UEFA-Club-Competitions-Online (research sub-agent).
- League formats vary by country beyond a double round robin: championship and relegation splits (Belgium, Scotland, Austria), relegation play-offs (Germany, France), promotion play-offs (England, Spain), spring-to-autumn calendars in the Nordic leagues, different winter breaks, cups with or without extra time and two-legged ties, and a FIFA calendar that merges the September and October international windows from 2026. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_International_Match_Calendar and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_football_league_system (research sub-agent).
- Historical league and cup results exist as public-domain JSON in openfootball on GitHub, as free CSV with unclear commercial terms at football-data.co.uk, and as CC BY-SA tables on Wikipedia; RSSSF is the deepest archive and offers no bulk export. Source: https://github.com/openfootball/football.json (research sub-agent).
- Real names reach football games through collective licences (FIFPRO for player image rights, plus league and club licences) or through community real-names files that the player imports into a game that ships fictional names. The import pattern has run for over a decade without a reported successful lawsuit, but no court has tested it, and a broken rights chain cost EA about 100 million dollars with Brazilian players. Source: https://www.fmscout.com/a-fm26-real-names-license-fix.html and https://www.sportcal.com/news/ea-sports-and-fifpro-in-long-term-licensing-deal-extension/ (research sub-agent).

## What we are assuming

### Still open

- A referee who leans towards the home side reads as realism rather than as the game cheating the player. Published research now measures that leaning (in fouls, cards, and added time) and ties it to the crowd, so the game copies a real effect rather than inventing one.
- Flight arcs, heading duels, and spin on every ball update fit inside the 10 percent CPU tripwire the engine build records. Tracking data puts the ball above 1 metre for 12 to 19 percent of frames, so the aerial path is not a rare branch.
- A realism budget measured against the shipped engine can be chosen before the additions are built, and the engine stays fast enough to run 1000 calibration matches inside it.
- The engine can model the duel as a first-class contested action at roughly 456 a match without breaching its performance budget.
- Each action family gets its own resolution model, with the action kind and the situation as the first inputs and the player's attributes and mood as the second, so no single family carries player quality for the rest.
- A hand-built choice model (the release mix by zone and pressure, a shooting threshold by distance, and a four-term receiver score with a pressure modifier) reproduces the measured shooting threshold at the edge of the box and the preference for the safe open receiver closely enough, without a learned model.
- A generated backstory produces a history that holds together: a club's past results, its partnerships, and its reputation agree with each other and with the squad on the books.
- The cost of changing the event contract after the viewer, commentary, and report parts of the engine build are built is acceptable, and it is smaller than the cost of disturbing a complete part of the build today; every consumer of the event stream being still unbuilt today is the reason to doubt it.
- Public data on real careers is enough to size age curves per attribute and the player-to-player variance around them, even though real data measures performance rather than attributes.
- A real age curve measured on performance converts to an attribute curve through the same deliberate amplification the game applies to attribute effects (made larger than their measured sizes so that a management game rewards a better player), so a player's decline reads on the pitch at the size real careers show.
- Moments are one source of the player-to-player variance that real career data measures around the age curves, not an extra spread on top of it, so the curves as a whole still match real careers.
- A result-only model can be calibrated to the full engine's results (scorelines, player totals, injuries, and development inputs such as minutes and moments) closely enough that a simulated past reads as the same football as the present.
- Training, coaching, and personality effects on development are set by design rather than by data, because no public data sizes them, and the career test bands only the factors the public research measures: playing time and level from the Transfermarkt career data, age peaks, physical curves, academy and loan outcomes, and ACL recovery.
- The spread of loan outcomes can be set by design, because the Bundesliga loan study measures only the average effect of a loan and not the spread around it.
- A valuation built from visible data only can be banded directly against the 650,000 Transfermarkt valuations in the public career dataset, because both read the same kind of visible inputs.
- Owner types and financial rules together reproduce the measured shape of club losses (most clubs losing money and owners covering it within the rules) without a band that forces the share of loss-making clubs.
- Real player and club names, likenesses, and badges can reach the game through a database the player imports, so the game itself ships no data that needs a licence.
- The result-only model can run the leagues of all 55 UEFA member associations at once without making each matchday wait, because only the leagues the player chose run the full engine.
- The team generator can produce believable competence ratings for every position without a scouting or history model behind them.
- The two sources of disagreement about a player's value, scouting noise and each club's own weighting, can be calibrated together, so the spread of transfer fees bands cleanly even though one valuation can be wrong for two reasons at once.
- Building the match-rules and tactics-and-ai slices against a player record that will change under them costs less than delaying both slices to change the record first.
- Consistency can be an attribute that narrows a player's match-to-match spread without adding team-level variance, so a player repeats while the team's goal distribution stays Poisson.
- The degree by which consistency narrows each action follows the order in which real players repeat (duels and physical output most, passing next, finishing and saving least), so the departure from the measured skill sizes is largest where the real data repeats most.
- A weaker-foot rating can give two-footed players a visible edge while real rarity and a small effect size keep two-footedness from dominating squad choices.
- A human can judge whether a simulated season reads as football from its table, its top scorers, and its transfer list, without watching any match inside it.
- Goal-scoring in the engine can reproduce a Poisson distribution of total goals while mood, personality, and player quality all move the scoring rate.
- Testing the physical bands (distance covered and time at each speed per outfield player, and the spread of peak sprint speed across a league) requires the engine to report per-player distance and speed, which the engine does not report today.
- Ball height costs the engine little, because crates/engine/src/ball.rs already carries a third axis and a third velocity component, so the work is in the rules rather than in the state.
- The realism test bands each action family against one named provider definition, StatsBomb for most on-ball and goalkeeper actions, SkillCorner for runs and pressing, and DFL tracking for carries, shielding, and goalkeeper positioning, rather than mixing definitions within a band.
- The deliberate amplification of player effects can be confined to player-level outcomes, so the league bands (goals, home advantage, and per-match rates), the Poisson scoreline, and the club-to-club spread band still pass while only the per-player residual band fails.
- One amplification size exists that is large enough for a manager to see a better player over about five matches and small enough for the best player's season totals to stay inside the real range.
- The realism test checks the style clash by fitting the measured possession rule (match possession equals 0.487 plus 0.949 times the difference in the two clubs' season means, plus 0.027 at home) across every simulated match with AI managers on both sides, and the fitted multiplier lands near 0.949; the person gave no preference on the test, so this follows from the decision that the rule is an output of the matches and not an input, rather than from a choice.
- Each StatsBomb definition has a DFL equivalent close enough to convert a band, and 7 DFL matches are enough to check the conversion even where they are too few to measure the band.
- DFL tracking at 25 frames a second converts into a fixture of 50-tick frames by interpolation, with player identities mapped to shirt numbers, and the viewer shows it without a change to the viewer.
- The realism bands measured on StatsBomb open data can still calibrate a commercial game, because calibration uses derived statistics rather than shipping the data; StatsBomb's research framing makes this a question for StatsBomb, not a settled right.
- Adding more detail inside the planned model produces the emergent football that is missing when the numbers pass but the match still does not read like football.
- A manager-set trigger can live in the rule pack and the change queue, so no conditional tactic logic enters the engine's tick loop.
- Nine paired phases can be derived from the engine's own state rather than authored by the manager, so the tactic interface stays at two phases while the model runs nine.
- A team's block can be made to track the ball at the fixed measured ratio (about 0.63 metres up the pitch and 0.30 to 0.39 metres sideways per metre the ball moves) without explicit per-player marking logic in the tick loop.
- The AI manager's live decisions enter the match through the same change queue as the human's, so both sides change tactics the same way and no tactic logic runs inside the tick loop; this extends the assumption about manager-set triggers to every live change.
- A per-player transition default can be derived from the team's two transition choices and the player's role and attributes, so a manager who never opens the per-player screen still gets a coherent team transition.
- The engine can keep club identity, squad, and tactic as separate inputs to style, so the realism test can check a club-to-club spread band and still tell which layer produced the spread.
- AI managers that pick tactics from their four inputs (philosophy, opponent, match state, and season situation) produce, over a season, choices whose results reproduce the measured possession rule with its 0.949 multiplier, so the rule emerges as an output without being tuned directly.
- The three effects of the tactical attribute (information accuracy, learning speed for a new tactic, and the squad's mood and trust response) apply to AI managers as well as to the human, so both sides of a match are modelled the same way and only the source of the choices differs.
- A player of the game can tell mood-driven errors from an ignored instruction, because the squad's mood and trust are visible to the manager before and during the match.
- The other matches of a matchday fit in the background of a real-time match on the player's machine without slowing the player's own match or its view.
- A player of the game reads a habit that overrides an instruction under pressure as realism, because the match view or the report names the habit and the moment, and not as the game ignoring the manager. This is, in a narrower form, the same risk as the rejected bet that players would accept instructions landing weaker under a weak manager.

### Confirmed

- Whether a manager may keep a hurt player on the pitch is still unanswered; giving injuries a severity and a risk driven by play settles severity and risk, but not the manager's choice.
- Personality fits the existing 1-to-100 attribute scale and needs no new data type.
- A milestone review that a person performs by hand catches what the shape measures miss, and it happens early enough to catch a failed mechanism before the rest of the model is built on it.
- Real football publishes a distribution of scorelines and a team-to-team spread that the test can band against, the same way it published the three averages in the planned realism bands.
- The plans already in progress are the floor, so every idea on this board adds scope and none removes planned scope.
- The charter of the football-manager-match-engine workflow still describes that workflow correctly, although the product is now read as a management game and the charter records exclusions (leagues, seasons, competitions, transfers, and finances) that the product no longer honours.
- The manager is a modelled entity with attributes, and not only the AI module the shape document already names.

### Rejected

- The match engine ships first and the season layer builds on it, so no season requirement changes the engine's data contract after the fact. Rejected, because the decision to add the player identifier to the event contract when the season layer starts changes that contract after the fact on purpose.
- Per-player match ratings can be derived from the event stream the engine already emits. Rejected by a read of the code at the time, which found no player identifier on the match event.
- The realism programme still fits the earlier decision that every idea on this board belongs to one second workflow, separate from the match-engine build, although the full season layer (table, availability, calendar, and cups) makes that workflow larger than the match engine it follows. Rejected when the work was split into two programmes, engine realism and the season layer.
- One past season is enough history for a partnership to read as earned, so a pair with one season of shared minutes is distinguishable from a pair with none. Rejected when the save moved to several simulated past seasons.
- A transfer market and finances can be built on the attributes that the work to deepen the player model supplies, with no scouting model, no contract-negotiation model, and no separate player-value model.
- A duel resolved from attributes, mood, and ball state reproduces the real pass-completion curve by kind of pass (from 91.7 percent for a simple pass down to 30.7 percent for a cross) as an emergent result, so no probability table for each kind of pass is written by hand.
- Attribute effects sized to the spreads measured in real football, about plus or minus 7 points of pass completion and 5 of shot conversion between the best and worst players, still read to a manager as real differences between players.
- The three aggregate calibration bands (goals, shots, and possession) are a sufficient test of realism.
- The three calibration bands still hold once mood and personality move decisions; the extra variance does not push goals, shots, or possession outside them.
- Aggregate shape measures can detect whether mood and personality read as football, and no human watches a match as part of the test.
- The engine can carry a provider-grade event contract with a core of five fields (player, team, x, y, outcome) on every on-ball event, adding per-kind qualifiers only for the kinds the realism tests read, rather than reproducing all 139 attributes of the official DFL schema.
- Form, season context, and a club's reputation for late goals can be supplied as asserted numbers in the team file, with no season model behind them. Rejected: these inputs now come from the season layer.
- Partnership data for each pair of players can be generated for a squad of 22 without a shared history to derive it from. Rejected: the season layer instead generates a backstory of past seasons when a new save is created, so partnerships start from a shared history.
- Players of the game accept that their own instructions land weaker when their manager is weak at tactics, and read the loss of authority as realism rather than as the game ignoring them.

## Tensions

### Still open

- Most realism bands are converted from 200 StatsBomb matches, and transfer fees and valuations are banded against the Transfermarkt-derived career dataset, but StatsBomb's open data is framed for research and the Transfermarkt set is exposed to Transfermarkt's own terms, so two of the test's largest sources may not be usable in a commercial game, while the event contract has already removed the same risk by using the game's own names with a mapping to outside schemas.

### Resolved

- Making pitch wear and weather interact pulled against the planned realism test of three aggregate bands held over 1000 matches: either the bands must hold across every combination of conditions, or calibration fixes the conditions and so never tests them. Resolved: calibration groups runs by condition band (dry, wet, worn) and sets a band for each, so a condition is expected to move the numbers rather than fail the test.
- Real per-match rates vary by only about one percentage point in pass accuracy and two shots in shot volume across five leagues, while the planned bands were goals 2.4 to 3.2, shots 8 to 16 per team, and possession 35 to 65 percent, several times wider than the real spread, so the test as planned would pass an engine that is visibly wrong. Resolved: four measured families (per-match rates, the scoreline distribution and home advantage, pass completion as a curve by kind and length, and physical output) replace the three bands, and the assumption that the three bands would still hold is rejected with them.
- Banding physical output and making the duel the place where player quality becomes an outcome both need the engine to report per-player data, but the event contract carries no player identifier, and the event-contract change (already repriced as a full redesign) is deferred to the realism programme, so the chosen test could not run against the engine the board had agreed to ship. Resolved: the test reads a separate test-only output with per-player data, so it runs against the shipped engine and the deferral holds.
- The findings that pressure matters only once pass purpose is separated, and that flat-pass completion is set by pitch position rather than by person, argued with how the board had framed player quality: completion is set by the kind of action and the situation first and the player second, so a uniform per-player attribute gives the wrong answer by role even with the right values. The duel already bet on emergence, nothing said the same for the pass, and the planned realism test could not tell the two models apart. Measurement then put the situational spread of pass completion at 18.3 points against a player spread of 3.6, with the same pattern for shooting and saving while duels are player-heavy, so the test can tell the models apart by banding the per-player residual spread. Resolved in favour of situation first and player second for the pass, the shot, and the save, with the duel keeping a larger player share.
- The duel had been made the single place where player quality becomes an outcome, while the person's correction says every action family is such a place, and the pass data already showed a completion surface a duel model does not produce. Resolved in favour of every action family: the duel stays a first-class contested action but loses the word single, and the bet that a duel model would reproduce the pass completion curve as an emergent result is rejected.
- The realism test reads a separate test-only output while the game reads the event contract, and the contract then became a full provider schema; two outputs report the same match, the test checks one and the player sees the other, so the test could pass a match whose reported events disagree with what it measured. Resolved: both outputs are written from one internal record with one definition per event kind.
- Merging DFL and StatsBomb into one contract pulled against the measured fact that providers count the same action very differently: where both schemas define the same event kind, the merged contract must pick one definition, and the promised benefit of output that any tool for that schema can read holds for neither provider in full. Resolved: DFL wins every conflict, so each shared kind has one definition.
- Making DFL the definition of every shared event kind pulled against banding most on-ball and keeper families against StatsBomb over 200 matches, because the public DFL set is only 7 matches, too few to band most families on its own definitions; either the StatsBomb-measured bands are converted to DFL definitions, or the bands are measured on 7 matches. Resolved: the StatsBomb bands are converted and the 7 DFL matches check each conversion, with the assumption that every StatsBomb definition has a close enough DFL equivalent carrying the risk.
- Simulating several past seasons in full at save creation pulled against the engine's cost: one league season takes about 20 seconds on eight cores with today's engine, so five seasons across five leagues is about eight minutes before the realism additions make each match more expensive; either save creation becomes a long wait, or the past seasons run a cheaper match than the one the player sees. Resolved: the past runs on a fast result-only model, with the assumption that it can be calibrated to the full engine carrying the risk.
- The career test bands injury after-effects against real data (two seasons below par, and 20 percent of professional careers ended within three seasons after an ACL rupture), while the choice to carry severe injuries more gently makes the dip shorter and ends no career; the injury band would therefore fail by design, as the amplified per-player band does, unless it is dropped or re-sized. Resolved: the injury band is re-sized to a softer target set on purpose.
- Keeping every match other than the player's own as a full simulation, with realism before speed, pulled against running the simulated past and every unchosen league on a fast result-only model; either those matches become full simulations, which the engine's measured cost prices at minutes per season before any realism addition, or full simulation covers only the active leagues. Resolved: full simulation is limited to the active leagues.
- Making a loan a career turning point that moves a player's curve pulled against the study of 378 Bundesliga loans, which found loanees gained no measurable minutes and no measurable market value; a game in which a loan develops a player departs from the only measured evidence on loans. Resolved when the person chose to let a loan help or hurt with an average effect near zero, which keeps the study true, with the spread of outcomes set by design.
- Giving a club four income sources pulled against the measurements that player sales are a main source of income in every league but the richest, at 29.5 percent of income in the Eredivisie; a selling club in the game could not earn what a real selling club earns unless transfer income became a fifth source. Resolved when player-sale income was brought into the club's finances, with the owner type deciding where it goes.
- Competence per position, new attributes, player mood, team mood, partnerships, cohesion, two tactical phases, set-piece routines, triggers, a full referee, interacting conditions, and ball spin all enlarge the cost of each engine tick, while the tactics-and-ai slice carries a benchmark tripwire that fails it at 10 percent more CPU time or 25 percent more memory than the match-rules baseline. Resolved: the tripwire guards only the engine build, and the realism programme measures against the shipped engine with a budget of its own; the assumption that such a budget can be chosen in advance and still run 1000 calibration matches carries the cost.
- Making weak players swing more and letting mood move decisions both widen match-to-match variance, while the board assumed the three calibration bands (goals, shots, possession) would survive that, and already doubted that three aggregate bands measure realism at all. Resolved: the realism test now bands the spread as well as the average (goalless matches, five-one results, and how far a team's worst match sits from its best), so it measures what those two mechanisms actually add; this rests on the assumption that real football publishes such a distribution to band against.
- A transfer market with finances needs a player value and a scouting judgment, but the plan to deepen the player model into a person showed a manager every attribute in full; when every club sees every attribute exactly, no club can value a player differently from another, so the market has nothing to trade on. This was the reason to doubt the assumption that a market needs no scouting model. Resolved: clubs now see a noisy scouting estimate and weigh attributes by their own identity, the no-scouting assumption is rejected, and the assumption that the two sources of disagreement can be calibrated together carries the cost.
- Making weak players swing more and letting mood move decisions both widen match-to-match variance, but real football's total-goal distribution is Poisson with a variance-to-mean ratio of 1.035, so the real variance is already fully explained by the scoring rate. Extra variance would move the engine away from real football unless the mechanisms shift the rate rather than the spread. Resolved: mood, personality, and quality now move the team's scoring rate and never the spread, so goals stay Poisson; the assumption that the engine can do this carries the cost.
- Two measurements of how far each line advances when a team wins the ball disagree: SkillCorner gives the back line 3.5 metres of advance and the wide roles 9 to 12, while the DFL multi-camera feed gives the back line minus 1.4 metres and the wide roles 5.4 to 7.4. The likely cause is the possession label, because SkillCorner marks controlled phases while the DFL feed marks ball ownership on every frame, contested ones included. Resolved in favour of the DFL figures, which are ground truth and labelled by the provider; the SkillCorner figures are kept for their ordering only.
- The person set the strong-versus-weak gap as errors, ceiling, and consistency together, and the plan for the player model builds attributes a manager is meant to feel. Real data shows that once the situation is removed a player moves pass completion by only about 3.6 points, shot conversion by 2.5, and save rate by 2.4, and that finishing and saving barely repeat within a season. An engine faithful to that may make attributes feel as though they do not matter; an engine that makes them matter will fail it. Resolved: attribute effects are amplified for the game and the per-player residual band fails by design, the assumption that measured sizes would still feel real is rejected, and the cost falls on the assumption that the amplification stays out of the league, scoreline, and club bands.
- Putting part of the quality gap in the repeat, so that a strong player has fewer bad days, argues with the measurement that finishing and saving barely repeat within a season, and with the chosen band on how far a team's worst match sits from its best; the assumption was that a consistency attribute could narrow a player's spread without moving the team's. Resolved: consistency acts on every action but by a different degree for each, which makes the departure from real data deliberate and graded, and a further assumption names the order the degrees follow.
- Hiding a player's true attributes behind each club's scouting noise lets clubs disagree, but basing every transfer fee on a shared market valuation computed from the true attributes would tell every club the truth that scouting hid, so either the valuation comes from something else or the disagreement shrinks to each club's premium alone. Resolved: the shared valuation is built from visible data only (age, minutes, level, goals, recent fees, and reputation), so it never leaks the true attributes, and it is banded against real Transfermarkt valuations.
- The new pressure-handling and sportsmanship traits describe much the same behaviour as the composure and aggression attributes the schema already carries, so the engine would either read two values for one behaviour or have to say what each value moves that the other does not. Resolved: each pair splits by scope, with the attribute acting on each action and the trait on occasions and choices.
- Twelve hidden traits on the attribute scale, plus the wish for new performance attributes, met a schema that accepts 30 to 50 attributes in four fixed groups with 13 slots believed free; the traits alone would take 12 of the 13 and leave one slot for every new performance attribute. Resolved: personality becomes a fifth group with its own slots outside the limit.
- Habits of play that act on their own, such as cutting inside, pull against the rule that the manager's instruction is always carried out as given: when an instruction to stay wide meets a habit of cutting inside, either the habit yields, which makes habits vanish under any instruction, or the habit wins, which weakens the instruction. Resolved: a strong habit wins sometimes, under pressure or low mood, and visibly, which amends the rule; the risk is that a player of the game reads this as the game ignoring the manager rather than as realism.
- Per-player form pulled against an event contract that carried no player identifier, and the stream-protocol slice was already complete with four slices depending on it, so per-player form needed the same kind of costly contract change already named for the player record. Resolved: the event contract changes when the work to build the season layer starts, with the assumption that the later migration cost is acceptable; the decision covers the event contract only, so the question of when the player record changes stayed open.
- Transfers, wages, and budgets are part of the product, yet the only realism test was the match bands with their shape measures, so nothing tested whether a market, a wage bill, or a season of results reads as football and the largest part of the product had no realism test. Resolved: the season test takes all three measures, table shape, market and wage shape, and a watched season.
- The season test put a human in the loop while the match test kept a human out, yet the argument that justifies watching a season justifies watching a match. Resolved: the assumption that aggregates alone can judge a match fell, and a person now watches matches too, as a milestone review rather than a gate on every build.
- The board handled a deliberate departure from real football two ways: the per-player band kept real data and failed by design under the amplification of player effects, while the injury band moved to a softer target and passed, so a reader saw one choice as a red failure and the other as a green pass. Resolved: every departed band carries a real target and a design target, passes on the design target, and reports its distance from real data.
- One reading treats the planned model as the right shape that only needs more detail; another says a fully detailed model can still pass the numbers without reading like football, so more detail does not imply emergence. The proposed bridge, personality deciding how strongly the player's own mood and the team's mood change that player's decisions, was never tested; putting a human in front of a match as a milestone review made it checkable, on the assumption that such a review catches the failure in time. Resolved by a principle rather than a proof: every realism band is an output the mechanisms must produce, never a tuned value, a failed watched-match review reopens this tension, and the bet that more detail produces emergent football stands.
- Form, season context, and a manager who moves form all need the season model that the engine build workflow excludes. Resolved: a season layer came into scope and was placed in a second workflow, so the assumption that form and season context could be supplied as asserted numbers in the team file was rejected, and the numbers are earned rather than asserted.
- Giving each player a competence rating for every position changes the shape of the player record, the data-schemas slice that defines that record is already complete, and the realism workflow starts only after the engine ships; the change is cheapest before the engine is built on the record, so this order pays a migration cost instead. Resolved: following the precedent set for the event contract, the record changes when the realism programme starts, on the assumption that migrating the match-rules and tactics-and-AI slices later costs less than delaying them now. The question of when the player record changes shape is answered and needs no investigation run.
- Putting all the realism work in one second workflow that starts after the engine ships pulled against making that workflow a full football-management game with a calendar, cups, a table, and season-long availability, larger than the engine it was meant to follow. The assumption that the season layer would never change the engine's data contract also failed, because team and per-player form and the combined club calendar and squad availability both want data the engine does not emit. Resolved: the work splits into two programmes, engine realism and the season layer, which rejects the assumption that one second workflow still fits; the no-contract-change assumption is rejected too, because the event contract is now changed after the fact by design rather than by accident.
- The product is a football-management game whose first component is the engine, while the engine workflow's own charter excludes leagues, seasons, competitions, transfers, and finances; the workflow in flight was building to a charter the product has outgrown, and nothing had told it. Resolved: the charter stays as it stands and the engine's definition of done is unchanged, because splitting the work into two programmes makes both descriptions true at once, which confirms that the charter still describes its own workflow. The residual cost is that a reader of that workflow alone will take the engine for the whole product.
- The event-contract change was deferred to the realism programme and priced as adding a player identifier, but real provider data puts its true size at roughly fifteen event types instead of four (a real match carries about 1683 events; Wyscout uses 10 types and about 35 subtypes and kloppy's model 19 types), plus a player identifier, coordinates, and an outcome on every event. The deferral was priced as one field and is really a redesign of the contract. Resolved: the programme accepts the full redesign and copies a real provider schema in full, so the core-subset idea (player, team, x, y, and outcome with a few per-kind qualifiers) is rejected and the cost is priced at its true size.
- The realism test bands league-wide per-match rates to about one percentage point, yet the club-to-club spread on the same statistic is 17 points; because the test adopted the league bands alone, it would pass an engine in which every club plays identically, and nothing on the board produced club variation of that size. The measured size of team strength and the split of club style into a strength axis and a pure-style layer supplied the band, and it was resolved by building club style from three layers (identity, squad, and tactic), at the cost of keeping those layers separate in the engine.
- Adding the manager's match tactic on top of the measured additive possession rule, with the rule banded as the default, pulled against how the rule was measured: the 0.949 multiplier comes from real clubs whose managers already chose a tactic for every match, so an engine that adds a tactic on top counts that adaptation twice. Resolved by making the rule an output of the matches rather than an input to them, so no tactic is counted twice.
- Both managers carry attributes, and a weak tactician is one who makes poor choices; the AI manager's choices come from its attributes, but the human player makes the human manager's choices, so the human manager's tactical attribute had nothing left to act on. Resolved by giving the attribute three things to act on (information accuracy, learning speed, and squad reaction), none of which is the instruction.
- The rule that an instruction is always carried out as given removes every path by which a manager weakens an instruction, yet the manager's attribute moves squad mood and trust, and team mood gates the error rate, so a weak manager's team makes more errors after the same instruction: the removed weakening arriving by a longer road. Resolved by reading those errors as mood rather than a weakened instruction, so the rule stays true; the remaining risk is whether a player of the game can tell the two apart.
