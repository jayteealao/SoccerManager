    The Brainstorm

    You started from one thought. The in-progress plans for Touchline build a lawful, tactical, calibrated match, and you wanted to know what they still miss on realism. Six sessions later, the answer is a whole product. Touchline is a football-management game, and the match engine is its first component. The engine workflow keeps its charter and its sixteen slices. The realism work runs as a separate programme after it, measured against the shipped engine with a performance budget of its own.

    What we believe now, area by area:

    • The player. A player becomes a person. The single position code becomes a competence rating per position, and each foot gets its own rating. Players gain new performance attributes, a personality with hidden traits, and habits that training can change. Under pressure or low mood, a strong habit can override an instruction, and the manager can see why.
    • The club. On the pitch, a club’s style comes from its squad and its tactic. Club identity acts off the pitch: it shapes who the club hires and buys, and what the board and fans expect, blended with the owner type. A club also has a home ground, a reputation, and a team mood that personality shapes.
    • Tactics. Each team has an in-possession shape and an out-of-possession shape, set-piece routines, and live changes that wait for the next stoppage. Both managers can make those changes.
    • The match. The engine resolves every kind of action from the situation first and the player second. It uses the height the ball already has for play in the air. It has a full referee with a video assistant, and injuries have a severity. The player’s own match plays in real time or as key moments, and the other matches of the day run beside it.
    • The realism test. The test measures against real match data, and its bands are far tighter than the plan’s first guess. Where the game departs from real data on purpose, the band has a real target and a design target. People watch matches and seasons at milestones to review them. The test keeps its StatsBomb and Transfermarkt sources: you accept the licence risk, because the game ships none of the data. Cups get one band of their own, the upset rate by the gap in divisions.
    • The season. The season layer mirrors the real European structure. It adds careers with age curves, a market that values players from visible data only, and a club business with a board and an owner who can sack the manager. The fast model that runs the other leagues is set out under engine structure. A new save may take a few minutes to create. Money is kept in euros inside the game, and the player chooses the currency on screen.
    • The staff. Staff are people who also carry a rating, and each club sees that rating through its own judgment. Every role a real club employs is in the game, from coaches and scouts to the groundsman, the psychologist, and the loan manager. Each one has attributes, a personality, specialisms, a playing idea, a contract, and a market. Numbers and quality differ by league and tier. Staff are full people only in the active leagues, and the player chooses per department whether to hire directly or to delegate. Staff speak through reports, a word during the match, and meetings. Their advice can be wrong through noise, a personal leaning, an agenda, or a blind spot. Overruling them has a cost, and the game remembers who was right. Staff follow a manager through an inner circle and their own loyalty, other clubs can poach them for a fee, and they have careers: retired players become coaches, and coaches become managers. Staff know some places better than others, form bonds with players, tire under too much work, and need facilities the club pays for. On the pitch, staff decide how fast the squad learns set-piece routines and tactics, how the team fades late in a match, and how well an opposition plan lands.
    • Contracts and the dressing room. A contract is negotiated between the agent, the director of football, the manager, and the player’s family, and the player weighs the role, the project, and life off the pitch as well as money. Every agent is a person, but only a few large agencies hold real power. A contract carries money, bonuses, clauses, and promises; most promises are verbal and move trust, and a few are written. The years left on a contract lower the price, and a release clause caps it. The dressing room has standing, groups, a culture, an icon, and a view of the manager. Unrest spreads through leaders and groups, escalates from a private word to a refusal to play, and has a small effect on the pitch. The manager disciplines within each country’s real rules, with warnings, fines, dropping, training apart, and public pressure, and strong standing at the club stretches those limits. A manager who crosses the line faces the board and a tribunal that can award anything up to a free exit.
    • Supporters and the press. Supporters are groups with their own expectations, and the club’s fan mood is the balance between them. Fans react to the club’s identity, and they hold power through the board, real ownership models, money, and the manager directly. The crowd moves the referee, nervous players, and the home team’s late belief. Rivalries come from the world data, geography, and events in the save. The manager attends every press conference, can delegate one, and picks the players where a competition requires them, as UEFA does. Stories build over weeks from real events and invented rumours. Journalists are people, and the manager can give exclusives, leak, freeze someone out, and learn who is reliable. How media pressure reaches the manager’s job depends on the owner.
    • Where new players come from. Academies follow each country’s real structure, and the full player model starts at the scholarship age, about 16; younger players form a rough pool that clubs learn about slowly through staff, time, tests, and other clubs’ interest. A young player’s future is a range that narrows, with no fixed ceiling. Clubs over-rate early-born players, as real academies do, and good staff can correct it. Players also arrive as late bloomers, through the lower leagues, and from beyond UEFA. The generator copies measured patterns: birth quarters, late maturers, growth over decades, each nation’s output, and dual eligibility. A player’s style comes from where the player develops, not from nationality. B teams and squad registration follow real rules by country and competition. The world may drift over a long save, and the season test keeps it inside real ranges.
    • International football. The calendar, national teams, and players on duty are in the game; managing a nation is a later addition. Call-ups follow club form, the national coach’s taste, loyalty, and the nation’s depth. The manager can play a player into a call-up or ask for rest, players choose between nations and can retire from duty, and duty brings fatigue, injuries with compensation, mood, and changes in value and fame.
    • The manager’s week. Team talks before, during, and after the match move mood and focus, and they depend on reading the room, on not repeating one approach, on words to single players, and on the manager’s standing. Delegation works the same way in every area. Information arrives in a daily inbox the player can filter, periodic reports from delegated work, and urgent escalations from staff. Reasons behind events show in a mixed way. How the manager’s time is limited is still open. Clubs can fall into crisis, and the realism test reaches off the pitch with bands for measured facts and design targets for the rest.
    • The data model. Every attribute keeps a named job in the engine. An action runs in stages, and each stage blends a main attribute with a few supporting ones, weighted from real data where it exists. Each overlapping pair has its own split, and pace is held back three ways. Attributes are stored in tenths behind the familiar 1-to-20 display. A convex curve makes the top of the scale count more; its strength is set so a top player looks better in about two of three runs of five matches, and the same convexity prices development and the market. The world’s spread of ability is wide on purpose. One effective-ability layer moves a player within bounds through body, mind, familiarity, and surroundings, so an 18 has poor patches and hot streaks but never becomes poor or unstoppable. Consistency and injury proneness are hidden and shown in words, and personality traits use the same 1-to-20 scale. A player’s grasp of each tactic is personal, and the team’s familiarity is computed from the players on the pitch. About 40 roles each set where a player stands, what the player prefers, and how teammates use the player, and a duty scales the role. A sensitivity test of its own changes one attribute, setting, or role at a time and checks that only the related statistics move. Team numbers act on actions that need several players, such as pressing chains, and club identity acts only off the pitch, through hiring and through the pressure of the board and the fans. Every rating in the game, for staff, managers, referees, and agents too, uses the same scale. Height gives reach at a small cost in turning, age slows recovery, and a player new to a country adapts over months. A relationship between two people keeps on-pitch understanding apart from personal liking.
    • Action resolution. Each contact, meaning a tackle, a first touch, a keeper’s handling, a strike, a pass, or an interception, draws from a chance or a spread built from the situation and the attributes, and physics carries the ball between contacts. A strike takes two draws, quality first and then error. A keeper’s reach is physics and the handling a draw, and a penalty adds a guessing game. A duel runs in two steps and ends four ways. Errors come from three sources, the tails of the draws, concentration lapses, and a few named error events, and each source has a fixed share of the error band. The checks judge shots with an xG model fitted on real data, and band the shot split, pass curves, duels, Opta’s errors leading to goals, and season variance with real and design targets.
    • Calibration. Targets come from five recent seasons of real data (2021-22 to 2025-26) in three layers: each league with data, each tier, and banding groups for the other leagues. A division’s style emerges from its players, not from settings by hand. Each band’s tolerance is the real spread widened by sampling noise. The check compares every scoreline and whole distributions, not only averages, and bands the league table in full. Every report is stored with its version and compared with the last. Each change runs about 1,000 matches. Each milestone runs the touched parts of the heavy checks, and a full release run finishes overnight on a Mac mini and a VPS.
    • Tactical counterplay. Costs, counters, and squad fit emerge from play: a high line is exposed because a slow defender loses the race, and a press tires the players who run it. There is no counter table and no fit number. Rest defence follows the duties, and the gaps between the lines emerge from where roles stand. A round-robin of eight named styles with identical squads and a grid over the settings prove that every style has a clear weakness. AI managers read the same noisy staff advice as the human, remember what worked, and carry a tactical fingerprint that analysts can reveal. Paired squads prove that fit matters. Beyond the brief, the AI learns during a match and an exploit hunt runs before each release, players manage games themselves with the manager’s instructions on top, the manager can shout from the touchline, and conditions change which tactic works.
    • Engine structure. The full engine runs every match in the active leagues, and a fast model runs the rest. The fast model is refitted from the full engine after each change, and it must match the full engine both in season bands and per pairing of team strengths on a fixed batch. A written state machine names every phase’s entry and exit, and a check confirms where players stand at each restart. Grounds differ in size within the laws. Added time prices each kind of event, including video reviews. A match replays identically on every supported machine. A skipped match finishes on the fast model from its current state, background matches run first and reveal their events in step with the player’s match, players near the ball think on every tick while the others think at a human rate, and a mid-match save finishes on the engine version it started with.
    • Player decisions and off-ball movement. Each player works from a picture of the pitch that timed scans refresh, holding where others are heading and what team-mates call. A choice takes decision time and an action takes commit time, and pressure arriving inside either hurries or blocks it. The carrier chooses from the whole catalogue of 53 real actions plus game extras, scored as expected value with weights fitted to real choices; a poor decision-maker misjudges values and picks loosely. Without the ball, players move from an anchor through role patterns and an off-ball utility, and share space and defensive jobs by claims and calls, inside the team’s marking system and counter-press window. Positional instructions are limits, choice instructions are weights followed as faithfully as the player’s decisions and teamwork allow, and traits show as repeated choices and styles. Bands check shape, crowding, runs, pressure, handoffs, frozen players, and passes through crowds.
    • Output and presentation. The engine writes a log of true causes, and every figure, visualisation, commentary line, rating, and staff sentence comes from the one match record, is recomputed by a test, and traces back to its moments. Figures are always true; staff readings and advice can be wrong by staff quality, and better analysts unlock more depth. Commentary is text with sound, from AI-written, reviewed banks that know player history, the season, the match’s own stories, and the causes. Ratings value every action and off-ball contribution, fairly for each role. Highlights rank by win-probability swing, moments the manager can act on, and story. A 180-item catalogue of statistics and visualisations fills designed, customisable screens, with a provider-style xG, one definition per metric, and plain names. Staff explain live, at half-time, after the match, and across matches. The playtest asks testers why a match was won or lost and checks their answers against the true causes.
    • In-game management. Substitutions and formation changes wait for a stoppage, while shouts and instructions start at once and spread through the players over time. The manager has team instructions, opposition targets, player instructions, and prepared game-state plans, and sees each player’s state, the analysts’ read of the opponent, what each change did, and the assistant’s costed proposals. The match pauses freely or on chosen events. Each competition has its own dated rule pack, concussion substitutes included where allowed, and the manager decides on a head knock after the medics advise. The AI manager has the same rules, information, and reaction time, and uses every tool as well as its skill allows. Every change has a cost, forked matches with paired seeds band its effect against real game-state data, and the exploit hunt tries sequences of changes. After a skip, the manager’s plans and triggers apply.
    • Set pieces. The manager builds routines from a library, an editor, and the set-piece coach’s proposals, names takers per kind and side, and sets defensive jobs within a zonal, man-marking, or mixed system. First contact comes from the delivery’s flight, each player’s run, height, and jumping, and a heading duel, with blocks and holding the referee may punish. Throw-ins include long-throw, retention, and quick throws. Routines need drilling, a squad holds only a few well, and opponents learn a favourite routine. Each routine’s rates are banded, the set-piece goal share has a band per league that may drift as the world copies what works, penalties are banded per league with a shoot-out pressure curve, and a coach’s effect stays inside the real spread.
    • Fatigue, injuries, and congestion. A player tires through a short sprint reserve, a slow reserve, and mental fatigue, and paces himself when low, but late tiredness stays as modest as real data shows. Between matches, condition recovers over about three days while an accumulated load, seen only through the staff, raises injury risk. Injuries come from a hazard on each tackle, sprint, collision, landing, and turn, with real types, layoffs, and recurrence, plus training injuries, at real frequency. Returns pass through phases, knocks carry over and can be hidden, and history raises risk by site. Congestion costs mostly injury risk, as in real football, and rotation is also pushed by squad morale, sharpness, the calendar, and staff advice. Training follows a daily rhythm and a preseason base, sharpness comes from matches by level and match-simulation sessions, travel adds jet lag, and illness can spread through a squad. Serious injuries offer treatment choices in which the player and agent have a say, and a return affects confidence and caution. Medicals and market values read injury history, and an injury crisis reaches the board, the players, and the press. Background leagues carry the same state. Season bands check counts, burden, types, recurrence, and the congestion effect on full-engine seasons.
    • Long-term integration. Each match feeds the wider game: young players grow from training, minutes at the right challenge, and good performances; form runs at two speeds; reputation and value follow level-weighted ratings and big stages; and the board, the fans, and income read results against expectation. Loops are dampened by modest, banded mood, complacency, opponents who lift, real league rules, wage pressure, ageing, poaching, and rivals who react, plus a soft pull in AI choices. Every match shows its stakes. A multi-season check bands title turnover, fortune churn, and promotion and relegation rates, and guards stop long-save drift.

    The player as a person: attributes, personality, body, positions

    • A player carries at least eight personality traits (professionalism, temperament, ambition and loyalty, leadership, determination, adaptability, sportsmanship, and pressure handling), each a hidden value on the 1-to-20 scale stored in tenths, and still more traits are wanted. Core.
    • The new performance attributes are marking, off-ball movement, set-piece skills (corners, free kicks, penalties, and long throws), and flair, which with 36 attributes today stays under the 50-attribute limit. Consistency joins the hidden values. Big-match nerve is left to the pressure-handling trait. Core.
    • Where an attribute and a personality trait overlap, they split by scope: the attribute acts on each action in a match (composure on a shot under pressure, aggression on a tackle), and the trait acts on occasions and choices (a final, a penalty shoot-out, whether to dive or to foul cynically). Why: Otherwise the engine would read two values for one behaviour without saying what each one moves that the other does not.
    • Four more traits join the eight already chosen, for twelve in all: versatility (willingness to play out of position), controversy (trouble off the pitch), money motivation (how much wages decide moves), and bravery (readiness for a 50-50, a header in traffic, or a block).
    • The player record gains a fifth attribute group, personality, with its own slots outside the 50-attribute limit, so the twelve traits stop competing with new performance attributes for the 13 free slots. The record changes when the realism programme starts, as the decision on when the player record changes shape allows. Why: The twelve traits alone would take 12 of the 13 free slots and leave one slot for every new performance attribute.
    • All four absent player fields matter: personality and traits; condition, morale, and form; age, height, and preferred foot; and positional familiarity.
    • A player carries a competence rating for every position the player can fill, not one position code, so picking a lineup is a trade-off rather than slot-filling. Why: Picking a lineup should be a trade-off between players rather than filling slots.
    • The gap between a strong player and a weak one is three things together: fewer errors, a higher ceiling of attempted actions, and more consistency. Why: Any one of the three on its own would read as a flat multiplier.
    • A player carries a mood that the match itself moves, and that mood drives the player’s decisions.
    • Personality decides how strongly the player’s own mood and the team’s mood change that player’s decisions; personality is the mediator, not a third mood.
    • Clubs disagree about a player’s worth for two reasons at once: each club sees a noisy estimate of the attributes, set by its scouting quality, and each club weighs the attributes differently by its playing identity and its manager’s attributes. Why: If every club saw every attribute exactly, no club could value a player differently from another, and the market would have no disagreement to trade on.
    • The player record changes shape when the realism programme starts and not before, following the precedent set for the event contract, which gains its player identifier only when the season-layer work starts. The match-rules and tactics-and-ai slices are therefore built against a record that will change under them. Why: It follows the same timing already chosen for the event contract change.
    • Attribute effects are amplified on purpose beyond the sizes measured in real football, where skill moves pass, shot, and save outcomes only a few points, because a management game must reward a better player. The per-player residual band, which the realism test gained once it could separate the situation from the player, is expected to fail by design. Why: A management game must reward a better player, and an engine faithful to the measured sizes may make attributes feel as though they do not matter.
    • A manager must see that a better player is better over a run of about five matches, through form and match ratings, rather than inside one match or only over a whole season.
    • Consistency narrows the spread of every action, finishing and saving included, but by a different degree for each action. Why: Real finishing and saving barely repeat within a season, so a graded effect makes the departure from real data deliberate rather than accidental.
    • Personality is hidden: the manager sees a description such as professional or volatile, whose accuracy is set by scouting quality and by time at the club, in the same way each club sees only a noisy scouting estimate of a player’s attributes.
    • A player carries at least four personality traits (professionalism, temperament, ambition and loyalty, and leadership), and more traits are wanted beyond these four.
    • Each foot carries its own rating, the strong foot is always 100, and two-footedness matters in play but must never become the dominant quality.
    • Four limits keep two-footedness from dominating: a good weaker foot is rare and costs more in the market; the weaker foot costs something only when the ball is on that side or there is no time to shift it; the gap between the feet grows with pressure and distance; and defenders and AI managers show a one-footed player onto the weaker foot.
    • A player’s rating for a position rises with minutes played there and with training for it over months, so a manager can retrain a player for a new position.
    • A player carries a few habits of play, such as cutting inside or shooting from distance, separate from attributes and personality, and training and coaching can add or remove a habit.
    • Under pressure or low mood a strong habit can override the manager’s instruction, and the manager can see why it happened. This amends the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given: it is carried out as given unless a strong habit overrides it at such a moment. Why: If a habit always yielded, habits would vanish under any instruction; if it always won, the manager’s instruction would be weakened.
    • A better player shows in four places: match ratings, statistics, visibly better choices and touches on the pitch, and staff reports whose accuracy depends on the staff’s own quality.

    The club and the squad: identity, venue, mood, cohesion

    • The club carries a home ground with a home advantage, a reputation and stature, and a playing identity. Tactic familiarity lives with the players, and the team value is derived from them. Core.
    • A team carries a mood of its own, separate from the mood of each player.
    • Team mood is moved by match events, by the score and the clock together, by the crowd and the venue, and by the context before the match.
    • Team mood is also moved by recent form, by the season so far, and by team context such as a club known for late goals or for slumps.
    • Team mood shifts how players behave inside the chosen tactic and gates the error rate; it does not change the tactic itself.
    • The squad has leadership, partnerships between pairs of players, and a cohesion value; cohesion is measured for the whole squad and again for the eleven on the pitch.
    • The measured additive possession rule is an output, not an input: tactic, squad, and identity drive each match, one match may land far from the rule, and the season results must reproduce it. This amends the earlier reading that applied the rule as each match’s default, so the default is now emergent rather than applied. Why: After a worked example, the person read the measured rule as the average: it was measured on real clubs whose managers already chose a tactic for every match, so adding a tactic on top of it would count that adaptation twice.
    • Until the season layer exists, each test match sets form, table position, and the season situation by hand, so the mood mechanism and the AI manager can be tested in extreme states such as a relegation fight. Why: The season layer that would supply these values does not exist yet.

    Tactics beyond Tier 2: phases, transitions, set pieces, live changes

    • The tactic has two phases, in possession and out of possession, each with its own shape and its own instructions, so the formation becomes two formations and the switch between them needs timing.
    • Set pieces are designed routines with assigned takers, and the opponent sets a defensive routine against them, so a set piece is a duel rather than a dice roll.
    • Tier 3 is per-player instructions and opposition instructions together, plus manager-set triggers for pressing, the defensive line, and similar situations. The person added the triggers themselves; they were not among the options offered.
    • The manager authors two phases and the engine derives which of the nine paired phases it is in from its own state, so the tactic interface stays at two while the model runs nine. Why: It keeps the tactic interface simple while the model still runs the nine phases that real data shows.
    • A set-piece routine changes four things: who wins first contact, where the ball goes (near post, far post, or short), where players stand for the second ball, and how many stay back against the counter. Why: Real corner data shows a set piece is decided by first contact, so the routine’s levers are sized around it.
    • The manager sets two team transition choices, to counter-press or drop back on losing the ball and to counter-attack or keep the ball on winning it, and each player carries an own transition instruction as a Tier 3 per-player instruction, which defaults sensibly from the team choices until the manager changes it.
    • The manager sees the nine derived phases after the match only: the post-match report shows time spent in each phase and how the team did in each, and the match view does not show the current phase.

    How the match plays: actions, choices, duels, the ball in the air, officials, injuries

    • The referee is a full official, with a foul tolerance, a card threshold, how often the referee plays advantage, how consistently the referee applies that threshold, and a leaning towards the home side that the crowd can move. Why: Asked whether the referee is a character, the person took the fullest option: a full official with a home leaning.
    • Pitch condition and weather both exist and they interact, so rain on a worn pitch is worse than either one alone. Why: Asked whether conditions exist, the person took the fullest option: pitch and weather, interacting.
    • An injury carries a severity, and its risk comes from what happens in play (hard tackles, fatigue past a threshold, a collision) rather than from a flat chance per minute. Why: The person took the fullest option over binary injuries; driving risk from hard tackles also finally gives the aggression attribute a real effect.
    • The aerial model supports flight arcs, heading duels, goalkeeper claims, and spin and swerve, so a player’s technique and preferred foot change where the ball ends up. Why: The person took the fullest option, using the ball height the engine already carries and that nothing yet uses.
    • The engine build’s benchmark tripwire (10 percent CPU and 25 percent memory over the match-rules baseline) guards the engine build against its own baseline and stops there; the realism programme measures against the shipped engine and sets a performance budget of its own. Why: The realism programme should pick a budget it can live in rather than inherit one written for a different purpose.
    • The duel is a first-class contested action with types for ground attacking, ground defending, aerial, loose ball, and tackle, resolved from both players’ attributes, their mood, and the ball’s height and speed. It was first framed as the single place where player quality becomes an outcome; the person later corrected that part, so the duel is one such place among many. Why: Asked whether the duel becomes a primitive, the person said yes, a first-class contested action; the data had shown the duel is the most common contested action and the engine modelled none.
    • Every action family is a place where player quality becomes an outcome, and the duel is only one of them: passes, through balls, positioning, off-ball movement, dribbles, shooting, goalkeeper actions, blocking and shielding, tackles and duels, and carrying and progressing the ball each need their own resolution. Why: The person corrected the board for treating the duel as the single place quality shows, naming the rest of the game; the data had already shown the pass has a completion surface a duel model does not produce.
    • The gap an attribute makes between players lives in four places at once: in the choice a player makes (the pass picked, the moment to shoot), in the duel, in the body (pace, strength, stamina), and in the repeat, so a strong player also has fewer bad days. Why: Having chosen to amplify attribute effects so a management game rewards a better player, the person placed the gap in all four places.
    • When the realism programme changes the event contract, the contract copies a real provider schema in full rather than a core subset, so the engine’s output can feed any tool that reads that schema. Why: The person accepted the true size of the contract change rather than a core subset, so the output could be read by existing tools for that schema.
    • The event contract mixes two providers: it keeps every DFL event kind and attribute and adds what StatsBomb carries that DFL does not, such as the freeze frame of player positions at each shot; this amends the earlier choice to copy a single provider schema in full.
    • The engine keeps one internal record of each match, with one definition per event kind, and both the test-only output and the event contract are written from that record, so they can differ in what they show but never in a fact. Why: The person asked for a worked example first, then chose one record with two views so the realism test can never pass a match whose reported events disagree with what it measured.
    • Where DFL and StatsBomb define the same event kind differently, the DFL definition wins, and StatsBomb only adds what DFL lacks. Why: Providers count the same action very differently, so a merged contract must pick one definition for each shared kind.
    • An in-match knock is handled by all three parties according to severity: the medical staff take a severe injury off at once; for a minor knock the player’s personality decides whether to play on, and the manager sees the knock and can overrule at the next stoppage; playing on raises the chance the knock becomes an injury and lowers the player’s output. Why: It answers the long-open question of whether a manager may keep a hurt player on the pitch, and the person confirmed this severity split as their reading.
    • A severe injury is carried across seasons more gently than in real football: a long absence, a shorter dip in form, and no career ended by injury, with personality and the medical staff moving a single player’s return around that average.
    • Full match simulation covers every match in the leagues the player chose; the simulated past and every other league keep the fast result-only model used at save creation and for unchosen leagues, so the rule that every other match is a full simulation amends neither. Why: Running every match everywhere as a full simulation would cost minutes per season before any realism addition, so the person limited it to the active leagues.
    • The referee’s home leaning, the rate of injuries during a match, and the effect of weather and pitch wear are all sized from published data rather than set by design, so the seventh build step needs a research pass before its design targets exist.     The realism test: bands, design targets, watched reviews

    • The realism test adds shape measures to the three aggregate bands: pass completion, where shots come from, possession in each third of the pitch, and goals by phase of play. Why: Three bands are not enough; with shape measures that the event stream already carries, a match that passes the bands and still looks wrong now fails something.

    • Calibration groups runs by condition (dry, wet, worn) and sets a band for each, so rain is expected to lower goals rather than to fail the test. Why: Pitch wear and weather interact, so the bands either had to hold across every combination of conditions or calibration would fix the conditions and never test them; a band per condition lets a condition move the numbers without failing the test.

    • The realism test reaches the season as well as the match: the shape of the league table, the shape of the market and of wages, and a watched season that a human reads and judges. Why: Transfers, wages, and budgets are the largest part of the product, and nothing tested whether a market, a wage bill, or a season of results reads as football; the person took every season measure offered.

    • A human watches matches as part of the realism test, as a milestone review rather than as a gate on every build. Why: The argument that justifies a person watching a season also justifies watching a match, and a watched match is the first instrument that can check whether a fully detailed model actually reads as football.

    • The realism test bands the spread as well as the average: how often a match finishes goalless, how often it finishes five-one, and how far a team’s worst performance sits from its best. Why: Weak players swinging more and mood moving decisions both widen match-to-match variance, so the test has to measure the spread those mechanisms actually add.

    • Mood, personality, and player quality change a team’s scoring rate within a match and never the spread around it, so goals stay Poisson given that rate; the rule that a strong player differs from a weak one by fewer errors, a higher ceiling, and more consistency, and the rule that personality decides how strongly mood changes decisions, are both mechanisms for the rate rather than for the variance. Why: Real football’s goal distribution is already Poisson, so the observed variance is fully explained by the scoring rate; extra variance would move the engine away from real football rather than towards it.

    • The realism test bands four things: the measured per-match rates across five leagues, the scoreline distribution and home advantage, pass completion as a curve by kind of pass and by pass length, and the physical output of players (distance covered, time at each speed, and peak sprint speed). Why: The planned three bands are several times wider than the real spread between leagues, so they would pass an engine that is visibly wrong; the person took all four families.

    • The best player’s season totals, such as the top scorer’s goals and the most assists, stay inside the range real leagues produce, so the season realism test bands them. Why: It caps the deliberate amplification of player effects from the season side.

    • The realism test reads a separate, test-only output in which the engine writes per-player data; the event contract that the game reads stays as planned, and the rule that the contract gains its player identifier only when the season-layer work starts still holds. Why: The chosen bands need per-player data that the planned event contract does not carry, and a separate output lets the test run against the engine that ships without bringing the contract change forward.

    • The realism bands measured with StatsBomb definitions are converted to DFL definitions, one mapping per shared event kind, and the 7 public DFL matches check each conversion. Why: DFL definitions win for every shared event kind, but the public DFL set is only 7 matches, too few to measure most bands on its own; converting keeps the large StatsBomb sample of 200 matches.

    • The first watched-match review runs as soon as the first new mechanism runs, for example the choice model for when to shoot and which pass to pick, and a review follows each mechanism after it, so a failed mechanism is caught before other work builds on it. Why: A review run by hand only helps if it catches a failed mechanism before the rest of the model is built on it.

    • The watched-match reviewer uses all three comparisons: a simulated match side by side with a real match replayed in the same 2D view, a checklist of named qualities (shape, tempo, decisions, set pieces), and open judgement of what looks wrong.

    • The realism test checks player development two ways: it bands what the research measured (peak age by position, speed of decline, conversion of youth players, and the after-effects of injury), and a person reads a set of simulated careers for what no band covers. Why: The person asked for the real data first, then chose to band what was measured and add watched careers, the same pattern as the season test.

    • The realism test checks finances two ways: it bands what the research measured (the revenue mix, the spread of broadcast income, league wage ratios, losses, transfer flows, and prize money), and a person reads a set of simulated club accounts for what no band covers. Why: The person asked for the real data first, then chose to band what was measured and add watched accounts, the same pattern as the career test.

    • The career test’s injury band is re-sized on purpose to a softer target, and the game is banded against that target rather than against the real after-effects of a severe knee injury (a mean 216 days out, two seasons below par, and 20 percent of professional careers ended within three seasons). Why: The game deliberately carries severe injuries more gently than real football, with a shorter dip in form and no career ended by injury, so a band on real after-effects would fail by design.

    • Every band that departs from real football on purpose carries two numbers, the real target and a design target; the test passes on the design target and reports the distance from real data beside it, and this applies alike to the per-player band broken by the deliberate amplification of player effects, to the band for how consistency narrows each action, and to the softer injury band. Why: Otherwise a reader of the test would see one deliberate departure as a red failure and another as a green pass, with nothing to say that both are choices.

    • Only build steps that change play get a watched review, so the first review follows team shape and the defensive block, and the test harness and the player record are checked by numbers alone; this answers the ninth open question of the engine realism design document.

    • The design target for the deliberate amplification of player effects is that a top player looks clearly better than an average player in the same role in about two runs of five matches out of three, close to real football, so a manager needs more than one run to judge; this sets the size for the rule that a manager sees a better player over a run of about five matches. Why: It stays close to real football while still letting a manager see quality over a few runs.

    • The usual top scorer and top assister stay inside the real range, and a record season beyond it is possible about as rarely as real record seasons happen; this sets how strict the limit on the best player’s season totals is, and the real range was still unmeasured when this was chosen.

    • The realism test keeps its StatsBomb and Transfermarkt sources and accepts their licence risk. Why: the test uses derived statistics, the game ships none of the data, and you are not worried about the risk.

    • The realism test bands the cup upset rate: how often a lower-division club beats a higher one, by the gap in divisions, measured from openfootball and Wikipedia results. Why: a cup tie is a match, so the match bands already cover play, and what cups add is the upset.

    • The realism test reaches the world off the pitch: it bands the measured off-pitch facts, such as sackings, manager tenure, the share of free transfers, and agent fees, and gives every design value a design target that a test checks, such as transfer requests per season. Why: a growing part of the game rests on design values, and a design value with no target is never checked.

This mechanism was later replaced by the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given, with a weak tactician making poor choices instead. Why: The person wanted both managers to carry attributes that matter, so that the player’s own competence is part of the simulation.
    • An AI manager picks each match tactic from four inputs: its own philosophy, the opponent’s strength and style, the match state by score and clock, and the season situation such as table position, a cup match, or a tired squad.
    • No instruction lands weaker because of the manager; a manager weak at tactics makes poor tactical choices and understands the match poorly instead. This replaces the earlier mechanism in which a weak manager’s instructions landed weaker, and the instruction a manager gives is always carried out as given. Why: The person corrected the earlier mechanism: there are no weak instructions, only poor choices, because a player told that their own instructions land weaker would read it as the game ignoring them rather than as realism.
    • The human manager’s tactical attribute acts on three things and never on an instruction: how accurate the manager’s information is (opposition reports, match analysis, and scouting noise, the same noisy estimate of attributes a club’s scouting produces), how fast the squad learns a new tactic (the squad’s familiarity value for each tactic), and how the squad’s mood and trust respond to the manager’s choices over time. Why: The human player makes the human manager’s choices, so once a weak tactician meant poor choices, the human’s tactical attribute had nothing left to act on; the person chose these three things for it.
    • A squad that distrusts a weak manager makes more errors after the same instruction, and that is mood rather than a weakened instruction: the instruction is carried out as given and the players carry it out worse, so the rule that an instruction is always carried out as given stays true. Why: The person judged it acceptable because the extra errors come from mood, not from the instruction itself.

    The season layer: competitions, careers, market, club business

    • Clubs can fall into crisis: takeovers, owners who run out of money, administration, and points deductions. Why: real clubs collapse and change hands, and a long save needs that risk.

    • A season layer comes into scope: fixtures, results, and form are modelled properly, so this board describes a product larger than the match engine. Why: Form and season context cannot be supplied as asserted numbers in a team file; they need a season model behind them, so they are earned rather than asserted.

    • The season layer contains fixtures and results, form, a league table with standings, season-long squad availability with suspensions, injury persistence, and carried fitness, and a full club calendar with cups, fixture congestion, and rotation.

    • Form is computed at two scopes, a team form and a per-player form, and the per-player form feeds the player’s mood, the mood that the match itself moves and that drives the player’s decisions.

    • A new save generates a synthetic backstory of past seasons, so partnerships, form, and club reputation start at believable values in the first match. Why: Partnerships between players cannot be generated for a squad without a shared history to derive them from, so the season layer supplies that history.

    • The event contract gains its player identifier when the work to build the season layer starts and not before; the engine ships on the contract it has, and the realism programme pays the migration across every part of the build that reads the event stream by then. Why: The stream-protocol part of the engine build was already complete with other parts depending on it, and changing the contract later was judged cheaper than disturbing a complete piece of work now.

    • The generated backstory is one past season deep: a form record, a final table position, and partnerships earned from minutes played together. This was later replaced by several fully simulated past seasons.

    • The season layer includes transfers and finances, with a market, budgets, and wages, so the product is a full football-management game rather than a match engine with a season around it.

    • A player’s ability changes over seasons along age curves, one per attribute, and the curves differ from player to player with the variance real players show in how they develop. Many other factors move the curves, and which factors they are is still open.

    • The manager runs a training plan between matches, setting focus and intensity, and training moves fitness, injury risk, the squad’s familiarity with each tactic, and player development.

    • Five factors move a player’s age curves: playing time and the level played at, training and coaching, injuries, personality, and moments. The list stays open, so other factors may be added later.

    • Moments are of two kinds and both move a player’s curve: match moments such as a debut, a decisive goal, or a costly error in a final, and career turning points such as a transfer, a loan, a new manager, or a first-team breakthrough.

    • A new save simulates several past seasons in full at save creation, so every player’s current ability, partnerships, and reputation agree with a simulated past. This replaces the earlier plan of a backstory only one past season deep. Why: One past season was judged not enough history for a partnership to read as earned.

    • The past seasons simulated at save creation run a fast result-only model instead of the full match, so save creation stays short while the present runs the full engine. Why: Running every past match on the full engine would make save creation too slow; a single league season already takes minutes on one core.

    • A loan’s effect varies widely with the club, the coach, and the minutes, and it can help or hurt, while the average effect across loans stays near zero, as the Bundesliga loan study measures. Why: It keeps the only measured evidence on loans true while still letting a single loan matter to a player’s career.

    • Each club has a board and an owner type, for example patient, ambitious, or frugal, that sets targets for league position, cups, finances, and style, sets how much patience the board has, and can sack the manager; sackings apply to AI managers as well.

    • A transfer fee is a shared market valuation plus a premium from each club’s own view, and the season test bands the resulting fees against real fees and valuations by age and position from the public Transfermarkt career dataset.

    • The shared market valuation comes only from what every club can see (age, minutes, level, goals, recent fees, and reputation), as a Transfermarkt value does, and it never reads the true attributes. Why: A valuation computed from the true attributes would tell every club the truth that scouting noise hides, and clubs would stop disagreeing about a player.

    • A sacked human manager keeps the save: the manager is unemployed, can apply for jobs, and receives offers based on reputation.

    • The player chooses at save creation which countries and divisions are active, so more leagues give more jobs and more clubs to deal with, at the cost of a slower season.

    • A club has four income sources: matchday income from stadium size, prices, and attendance; broadcast and prize money by league position and cup progress; commercial income from reputation and success; and owner money that the club’s owner type can put in or take out within limits.

    • Every league the player did not choose runs the fast result-only model used for the simulated past, so its players play, develop, and gain value, and the whole world moves while only the chosen leagues run the full engine.

    • Player-sale income enters the club’s finances, and the club’s owner type decides how much of it reaches the manager’s budgets and how much pays debts or wages. Why: Real selling clubs earn a large share of their income from player sales, and with only four income sources a selling club in the game could not earn what a real one does.

    • Leagues and competitions carry financial rules such as a squad-cost cap, and a breach brings a sanction: a fine, a transfer ban, or a points deduction.

    • The work to build the season layer splits into four cards: competitions (table, cups, calendar, congestion, suspensions), squad and careers (form, availability, training, development, the simulated past), the market (scouting, valuation, fees, transfers, loans), and club business (board and owner, income, wages, financial rules, sacking, the job market). Why: The season layer had grown too large for one piece of work, and smaller cards can each start on their own.

    • A later version of the game loads a real-life database whose players start at the current real season, possibly imported by the player, so the world must accept imported seasons; until then the player chooses how many past seasons a new save simulates.

    • Leagues, cups, and competitions mirror the real European structure in full, starting with the leagues of every UEFA member association, with their divisions, promotion and relegation, domestic cups, and European qualification.

    • AI clubs get the effect of training from their staff quality and their manager, with no day-by-day plan; only the player’s club runs the detailed training plan the manager sets between matches.

    • Manager sacking rates and board patience are sized from published data rather than set by design. Why: The person asked for it to be looked up, and then for every open data gap to be researched before the board continued.

    • Creating a new save may take a few minutes, with a progress view that shows the past seasons as they finish. Why: history depth and every UEFA league are worth a short wait at the start.

    • The money bands and the game’s accounts use euros, and the player picks the currency the screens show. Why: one currency inside removes the pounds-and-euros mix from every band.

    The staff around the squad: coaches, scouts, medical staff, as people with their own quality

    • Staff are people who also carry a rating: coaches, scouts and analysts, medical and sports-science staff, the assistant manager, a director of football, and other football roles beyond these. Why: every one of these roles changes what the manager knows or what the squad can do, and a rating gives a quick read of a person you still get to know.
    • Staff have a personality, with opinions, temper, and loyalty, so they can clash with the manager and with players. Why: a star rating with no person behind it feels wrong.
    • Staff have trade-offs: a coach has specialisms, for example good with young players and poor with veterans. Why: a rating that is always better removes the choice.
    • Staff have a market: they cost real wages, other clubs can poach them, and they can follow a manager to a new club. Why: staff that never move are not part of the football world.
    • Staff have a voice: they advise and warn, for example a physio who says a player is close to injury. Why: a real manager hears the staff before deciding.
    • The game also employs the wider staff of a real club: a sport psychologist, a nutritionist, strength and conditioning and rehabilitation coaches, a head groundsman, a team manager for travel and matchday logistics, a welfare officer and a translator for new signings, a loan manager, a head of recruitment, data scientists, and a set-piece analyst. Why: several of these roles act on mechanisms already on the board, for example the groundsman on pitch wear and the psychologist on mood.
    • Every staff role is a person, with attributes, a personality, a contract, and a career. Why: a department level would bring back the rating that feels wrong.
    • Staff are full people only in the active leagues. A club in a result-only league holds a light record of each staff member, which becomes a full person when it matters, for example when an active club hires that person. Why: full staff everywhere would break the save that starts in a few minutes.
    • Staff numbers and staff quality differ by league, division, and tier, as they do in real football. Why: a fourth-tier club with fifty specialists would not be real.
    • The player chooses, department by department, whether to hire staff directly or to delegate the hiring to the head of that department. Why: hiring fifty people by hand suits some players and not others.
    • Staff advice reaches the manager through three channels: written reports and messages, a word from the assistant and the analysts during the match, and regular staff meetings where heads of department raise concerns and proposals. Why: a real manager hears staff in all three ways, and advice only on request would hide the staff.
    • Staff advice can be wrong in four ways: random error that shrinks with quality, a personal leaning from personality that the manager can learn, the staff member’s own agenda, and blind spots outside the specialism. Why: real advice is wrong in personal ways, and learning each person’s leanings is part of the manager’s job.
    • The manager designs the set-piece routines and may delegate the design to the set-piece coach; the coach’s quality decides how well and how fast the players learn the routines and how many routines the squad can run properly. Why: the routine is the manager’s idea, but the coach decides how well it reaches the players.
    • Fitness and sports-science staff move how fast players tire and how much the team fades late in a match. Why: the match changes with the clock, and fitness work is the staff’s mark on that change.
    • Analysts find the opponent’s habits, and the quality of their work decides how well the opposition instructions land. Why: an opposition instruction is only as good as the analysis behind it.
    • Coaches drill the shape and the pressing, so a new tactic takes time to learn and good coaches shorten that time. Why: a tactic that works at full strength on the first day is not real.
    • Each staff member carries a playing idea: staff whose idea fits the club’s style get more out of the players, and staff whose idea clashes with the manager’s tactic work less well and complain. Why: real clubs hire staff to fit a style, and a new manager often brings staff who share the idea.
    • Staff follow a manager in four ways at once: each manager has a trusted inner circle that grows over a career; each staff member decides by personality and by the bond with the manager; the old club can block a move or ask for compensation while a contract runs; and the player’s own manager can bring trusted staff to a new club. Why: about three coaches leave with each sacked manager, and the movement is personal.
    • Staff can be poached: a staff member weighs club size, pay, role, and ambition; a club that takes staff under contract pays compensation; staff can leave for a promotion, for example to a head-coach post; and the player can approach other clubs’ staff under the same rules. Why: losing a good coach to a bigger club is part of real football.
    • Staff have careers: attributes grow with experience and fall with age; retired players can start as coaches or scouts and carry traits from their playing days; assistants and coaches can become AI managers, so the manager pool grows out of the staff; and staff build a reputation from results and the players they develop, which sets their market value. Why: staff are people, so they need a past and a future as players do.
    • Each club sees a staff member’s rating through its own judgment, so the rating can be wrong, as a scouting report on a player can. Why: staff are seen the same way players are, so no club sees the truth for free.
    • Staff carry four more things: knowledge of leagues, regions, and languages that sets where their judgment is sharp; bonds with players, who play better for coaches they trust and may follow them; a workload, so overloaded staff make more errors and a thin department fails in a busy season; and a need for facilities, since staff work better with good training grounds, data tools, and medical equipment that the club business pays for. Why: each of these is part of how real staff succeed or fail.
    • Overruling the staff has consequences: an ignored staff member can become unhappy and leave, players hear about clashes and the team mood moves, and the game remembers who was right, so trust grows or falls with the record. Why: a final word that costs nothing would make the staff’s voice empty.

    Contracts and the dressing room: negotiation, promises, agents, squad status, unrest

    • A contract is negotiated, with a story behind the signature, not filled in as a form. Why: a wage and a length typed into a form is the first thing that feels wrong.
    • A player weighs more than money when choosing a contract or a club: the role, the manager, the club’s project, and life off the pitch. Why: a player who counts only the wage is not a person.
    • A promise has a cost: when the manager breaks a promise about playing time, a signing, or ambition, the player reacts. Why: a free promise is an empty lever.
    • The dressing room has structure: leaders, groups, and a pecking order, so unrest can spread and can calm. Why: a flat squad cannot carry the mood the board already models.
    • A player’s standing in the dressing room comes from four sources: time at the club, ability and fame, a leader’s personality, and the captain’s armband that the manager gives, where a poor pick loses the squad. Why: real standing comes from all four, and each one gives the manager a different lever.
    • Groups form in the squad by language and nation, by age, by starters and bench, and by shared history such as the academy or joining together; a lone new signing can feel shut out. Why: groups are how a grievance or a friendship becomes a squad matter.
    • Unrest moves through leaders, who can calm it or spread it; through groups, first inside the player’s group and then beyond; into the press, which puts pressure on the manager and the board; and onto the pitch, where a divided squad plays worse together. Why: unrest that stays in one player’s mood is not how a real dressing room behaves.
    • The manager sees the dressing room three ways at once: some issues show at once, some arrive through people such as the captain and the assistant, and some show only as symptoms whose cause the manager must work out. Why: real managers see the room in part, and each issue reaches them by its own route.
    • A divided dressing room has a small effect on the pitch, smaller than the spread of skill between players, so it shows over a season and not in one match. Why: the measured skill spread is small, and a larger effect would make the dressing room outweigh football.
    • The squad holds a view of the manager, built from results, fairness, and the manager’s reputation. Why: respect is what gives the manager’s word weight in the room.
    • Senior players can go to the board, and a squad that turns on a manager can get the manager sacked. Why: player power is part of how real managers lose their jobs.
    • A club has a culture of standards and a winning habit that lasts across seasons; new players take it on or erode it. Why: a club is more than the squad of one season.
    • A club can have an icon, a player the fans and the board protect, whom the manager cannot easily drop or sell. Why: some players are bigger than the manager at their club.
    • Four parties shape a contract: the agent, whose skill and greed shape the deal; the director of football, who can negotiate for the club and to whom the player can delegate; the manager, who sells the role and the project; and the family, who can decide a move about country, language, or a child’s school. Why: a real deal has more than two sides.
    • A promise is verbal and moves the player’s trust and mood when it is kept or broken, and a few promises can be written as clauses, for example a pay rise after a number of games. Why: real promises are verbal, and only a few terms reach the contract.
    • The last year of a contract plays out four ways: some players run the contract down to earn more elsewhere, a player can agree terms with a foreign club six months before the end, clubs sell a player early rather than lose the player for nothing, and personality decides whether a player renews, runs down, or forces a move. Why: free transfers are a quarter to a third of big-five signings, so running down is a normal result.
    • The years left on a contract join the shared valuation, as in the CIES fee model, because every club can see them, and a release clause caps the price where one exists. This amends the earlier list of visible inputs to the valuation. Why: a player with six months left sells for far less, and free transfers are common.
    • A new signing paid more than the established players upsets the pecking order. Why: wages are visible in a real dressing room, and they carry status.
    • Bonuses change behaviour: a selfish striker with a goal bonus shoots more often, and a team bonus pulls the squad together. Why: a contract term that never reaches the pitch is only accounting.
    • The squad status in a contract sets how many matches the player expects, and every missed match counts against it. Why: squad status is the promise most players care about.
    • Some players play harder in the last year of a contract, and some coast after signing a long one, as their personality decides. Why: effort that follows the contract is a known pattern in real football.
    • An unhappy player escalates in steps: a private word, a formal transfer request, going public in an interview or a post, poor effort in training and matches that the staff notice first, and in rare cases a refusal to play that costs the player heavily in standing. Why: unrest needs a path to an end, and these are the real steps.
    • The manager’s discipline tools, such as fines, dropping a player, and training apart, work within the real rules of each country, and going past those rules brings disputes. Why: real football limits discipline, for example a fine of at most two weeks’ wages in England.
    • Real limits on discipline apply, but a manager with strong standing at the club can push further before a dispute starts. Why: it keeps real limits and still rewards a manager the club trusts.
    • The manager holds four kinds of discipline tool: a private warning, a fine within the limit, and a suspension for serious cases; dropping a player or sending the player to the reserve team; training apart from the first team; and public pressure through criticism in the press or the transfer list. Why: each tool lands differently on the player and the dressing room.
    • When the manager goes past the rules, the player can take a formal dispute to a tribunal and the club can lose and pay compensation, and the board can warn or overrule the manager to protect the club. Why: crossing the line must cost something the manager feels.
    • A discipline dispute goes to a tribunal that can award any outcome from a warning to a free exit with compensation, following the rules of the club’s country, and the players’ union appears only inside the dispute. Why: it keeps the real remedy without making the union a separate actor in the game.
    • Every agent is a person, with a personality, relationships with clubs, and an agenda, but most agents have little power; a few large agencies represent many players and deal often with the same clubs. Why: the research shows a concentrated market in which agents gain from moves, and a person with an agenda carries that better than a fee.
    • A contract carries four groups of parts: money (wage, signing-on fee, loyalty bonus, agent fee), performance bonuses (appearance, goal, clean-sheet, and team success), clauses (release clause, relegation wage cut, promotion rise, option to extend), and promises and status (squad status, playing time, a stated role, and promises about signings or ambition). Why: each part changes a player’s choices or a club’s money.

    Supporters and the press: fan mood, expectations, interviews, media pressure

    • Supporters are groups with a history, not an attendance figure and a mood bar. Why: a number cannot react to the club.
    • Supporters react to what the club stands for: selling the icon, abandoning the style, or raising ticket prices. Why: identity is what fans care about beyond results.
    • The press is not a quiz of stock answers: what the manager says has consequences. Why: repeated questions with no effect feel wrong.
    • Rumours, leaks, and headlines build over weeks into stories and sagas. Why: a single headline with no follow-up does not feel like football.
    • The supporter side has four parts: the crowd in the match, whose noise and pressure move home advantage, the referee, and nervous players; fan mood over a season, with expectations, patience, protests, and attendance; groups of fans such as ultras, season-ticket holders, casual fans, and fans abroad, who want different things; and rivalries and derbies that raise the stakes. Why: each part is a way real supporters shape a club.
    • The press side has four parts: press conferences before and after matches, whose words reach players, fans, the board, and rivals; journalists as people with sources, slants, and relationships; stories that build, peak, and fade over weeks; and media pressure that moves the manager’s job security and the players’ mood and form. Why: each part is a way the real press shapes a club.
    • The crowd moves three things: the referee, players on both sides whose personality handles pressure badly, and the home team’s belief late in a match when it chases a goal. Why: the data shows home advantage survives without the referee effect, so the crowd must reach the players too.
    • The manager takes part in every press conference and can delegate one to the assistant; where a competition requires players at a conference, the manager chooses which players go; and the manager, staff, and players meet the press in other situations as well. Why: press work is constant in real football, and choosing who speaks is part of it.
    • Stories come from real events in the game and from rumours that journalists invent, and the manager cannot always tell which is which. Why: real transfer gossip mixes truth and invention.
    • Supporters hold power four ways: through the board, which protests can move to overrule or sack the manager; through real ownership models such as member clubs with elections, the 50+1 rule, and supporters’ trusts; through money, as attendance, season tickets, and shirt sales fall; and through the manager directly, as chants and boos reach the manager’s standing and the players’ confidence. Why: real supporters hold all four kinds of power, and the kind differs by club and country.
    • Each fan group has its own mix of expectations, and the club’s fan mood is the balance between the groups. Why: ultras, season-ticket holders, and fans abroad want different things.
    • Rivalries come from three sources: known real rivalries in the world data, geography with stronger rivalries in the same city, and events in the save such as cup finals, title races, and a star who moves to the rival. Why: a rivalry needs a cause the fans recognise.
    • How media pressure reaches the manager’s job depends on the owner: some owners read the papers, and others listen only to fans or only to results. Why: owners differ, and the owner type already shapes the club business.
    • The manager can use the press as well as suffer it: give exclusives that buy goodwill, leak a story on purpose with a risk that it traces back, freeze out a journalist or an outlet with a risk of hostile coverage, and learn over time which journalists are reliable. Why: real managers manage the press, and each tool has a cost.

    New players entering the world: youth academies, youth intake, and generated players

    • Academies are modelled in full at least down to the under-18 level; below that, the younger players exist as a pool that clubs know only roughly, and over time a club learns which of them are likely to become good or great players; every player of the right age moves up through the age groups. Why: a full model of every child is not needed, but the path into the under-18s must be real.
    • The youth side has four parts: the academy with its grade, facilities, coaches, and age groups; recruiting young players through scouting, the local catchment area, and taking players from other academies with the compensation that follows; the intake into the professional ranks; and the path to the first team through under-21 and reserve football, B teams, loans, and a debut. Why: each part is a lever a real club pulls on its youth.
    • The world is free to drift over a long save, and the season test bands it, so the quality of the leagues stays inside a real range. Why: real football shifts over decades, and a test is a better guard than a generator that forces the same shape every year.
    • New players enter the world by four routes: club academies and their rough pool of younger players; late bloomers found in amateur or semi-professional football; players who rise through the lower leagues; and young players from beyond UEFA who arrive by transfer, held as a rough pool outside the modelled leagues. Why: not every professional comes through a big academy.
    • Generated players differ through the style of where they develop, bodies that follow real spreads by position, rare profiles at real rates, and background such as a professional parent or a choice between two nations; more sources of variety may follow the research. Why: players that look alike break a long save.
    • A young player has no fixed potential number: each player starts with a wide range of possible futures, and the range narrows as the player grows and the five development factors act. Why: a fixed ceiling from birth would make development a lottery ticket, and a range keeps the future open.
    • A club learns about its young players four ways: through youth scouts and coaches, as good as their own judgment; through time at the club; through physical tests, trials, and strong youth matches; and through other clubs’ interest, which is itself a signal. Why: real clubs learn slowly and from several sources.
    • The full player model starts at the scholarship age, about 16, or at the equivalent point in each country’s academy system; younger players form the rough pool. This refines the earlier choice of under-18. Why: the scholarship is the first real decision a club makes about a young player in every country.
    • Clubs over-rate early-born and physically early players and miss late developers, as real academies do, but good youth staff and a manager who knows about the bias can correct it. Why: the bias is real and measured, and correcting it is a skill worth rewarding.
    • B teams follow each country’s real rules: in Spain at least one division below the first team, in the Netherlands and Portugal with no promotion, in Germany no higher than the third tier, and in England an under-21 league and cup invitations instead. Why: real rules keep each country’s pathway distinct.
    • Squad registration rules belong: homegrown quotas by competition, each country’s limits on non-EU players and England’s work permits, and squad lists fixed at set dates; a researcher is finding the real rules before the detail is decided. Why: these rules make a club’s own academy worth more and shape every transfer.
    • The player chooses, as with staff hiring, whether to make the yearly decisions on young players (a professional contract, release, or a loan) or to delegate them to the academy head. Why: some players want to shape every youth decision and others do not.
    • The player generator copies four measured patterns: real birth-quarter shares with a small group of late maturers who have high upside, growth of about one centimetre per decade over a long save, each nation’s real output of players in total and per head, and eligibility for two nations at real rates. Why: each pattern is measured, and together they keep a long save believable.
    • A player’s style comes from where the player develops, the league and the academy, and not from nationality. This replaces national styles in the list of sources of variety. Why: league styles are measured, and the nationality differences in rating data may be stereotypes.

    International football: national teams, international breaks, call-ups, tournaments

    • International football must fix four things: breaks cost players fatigue, travel, injuries, and mood; call-ups follow a logic the manager can follow and influence; tournaments change players’ value, fame, and confidence; and national teams rise and fall, so a player’s nation matters to the club. Why: each of these feels wrong when it is missing.
    • International football has three parts in the game: the calendar of FIFA windows, summer tournaments, and club release rules; national teams with squads, coaches, rankings, qualifiers, and tournaments simulated as full competitions; and players on duty, with call-ups, caps, travel, fatigue, injuries, and effects on value, fame, and mood. Why: these are the parts that reach the club game.
    • A call-up follows four signals: club form and minutes weighted by league level, the national coach’s own style and favourites, loyalty to established internationals, and the depth of the nation, so a player from a small nation is called up at a lower level. Why: the manager can follow a call-up that rests on signals like these.
    • The manager and the player shape international duty four ways: minutes at the club raise the chance of a call-up, the manager can ask the national coach to rest a tired player and the coach may refuse, a player eligible for two nations chooses one and the manager or the family can sway the choice, and an older player can retire from international football. Why: each is a real lever around international duty.
    • International duty changes four things: fatigue from matches and travel, with long-haul trips costing more; injuries on duty, with compensation to the club; mood and confidence from a first cap, a good tournament, or being left out; and value and fame from a strong or poor tournament. Why: breaks and tournaments must cost and reward something.
    • Managing a national team is a future addition, not part of this programme. Why: it is a separate game mode that the club game does not need first.

    The manager’s week: information, time, team talks, and delegation

    • The manager gives team talks before the match, at half time, and at full time, and the words move the players’ mood and focus. Why: team talks are one of the manager’s most direct levers, and no session had named them.
    • The manager’s week is part of the game: how reports, messages, and decisions reach the manager, the calendar of training, travel, and press, and limits on the manager’s time. Why: every new system this session sends something to the manager, so the flow of information needs its own design.
    • Delegation works the same way everywhere: every decision area can be delegated, and the player sets how much to hand over. This joins the separate delegation choices for staff hiring, set-piece design, contract talks, press conferences, and youth decisions into one model. Why: five separate delegation rules would confuse the player.
    • The manager’s week must fix four problems: an inbox flooded with noise, time that costs nothing, days with no rhythm of match day, recovery, training, and travel, and events whose reasons stay hidden. Why: each makes the week feel unreal.
    • A team talk feels real when the right words depend on the score, the mood, and each player’s personality; when repeating one approach loses force and a rare outburst lands harder; when the manager can speak to single players; and when a respected manager gets more from the same words. Why: a talk that is the same pick every time is a routine, not a lever.
    • Information reaches the manager through a daily inbox that the player can filter, weekly or monthly reports from delegated work, and urgent issues that staff escalate at once. Why: it keeps the inbox familiar while cutting the flood at the source.
    • The reasons behind events are visible in a mixed way, as in the dressing room: some causes show at once, some come through staff, and some the manager must work out. Why: the same rule across the game lets the manager learn without being told everything.

    How the player watches the match: real time, key moments, other grounds

    • The other matches of a matchday simulate in the background while the player watches their own match, so the results are ready at the final whistle and live scores from other grounds appear as they happen.
    • Key moments come in tiers that together cover four kinds: chances and goals, moments the manager may act on (a tiring player, an injury, a card, a change of shape), a filter the manager sets, and the moments that tell the story of the result, including pressure that produced no shot.

    Cohesion across areas

    • An engine emulator, separate from the fast model, is fitted on a spread-out design of a few hundred input points with about 200 full-engine matches each, and predicts the mean and spread of every banded statistic from attribute levels, tactical settings, and engine constants. It has three roles: a tuning tool that proposes settings the full engine then confirms, a sensitivity screen that ranks which inputs move which statistics so the engine confirms only the ranked results, and milestone triage that predicts which bands a change moves so the milestone runs those parts. Accepted risk: It is reliable only inside its trained ranges, cannot find emergent effects such as a dominant tactic or an AI exploit, and is refitted after each engine change that moves the numbers, about 60,000 to 100,000 matches each time. A band always counts only on the full engine. Core.
    • A full release run must finish in about 12 hours on the Mac mini and the VPS together. When it does not, the run shrinks through pairing, early stopping, and sampling before any machine is rented. Accepted risk: If the realism work slows each match several times, overnight may need sampling that leaves some checks thinner before a release. Core.
    • Each milestone runs only the parts of the round-robin and the sensitivity family that its changes could affect, with paired seeds and early stopping. Everything runs in full before each release. Accepted risk: A change can reach a part the milestone skipped, and only the release run then catches it. Core.
    • The board studies two ways to shrink the check load: paired seeds, where the normal batch and the changed batch replay the same seeds, and early stopping, where a batch ends as soon as its result is clearly a pass or a fail. Core.

    The data model as an engine contract

    • The convex payoff lives in three places: on the pitch through the curve before the gap, in development where top points cost more to gain, and in the market where value and wages grow about 1.45 times per point of the level a valuer can see, never per point of a true attribute. Accepted risk: Three curves to tune, and their effects compound. Core.
    • Every one of the 36 attributes keeps a named job in the engine; none is cut or merged. Consistency and injury resistance keep their jobs as hidden values, and injury resistance becomes injury proneness. Accepted risk: Teamwork, work rate, and communication act on the team, so they need team-level mechanisms that do not exist yet. Core.
    • The sensitivity test is a test family of its own beside the realism test. Over a large batch it changes one thing at a time and checks that the related statistics move in proportion and unrelated ones stay still: one attribute on one player, one tactical setting, one role against a neutral default, and one team number such as cohesion or a partnership, which must move only team actions. When and how often each part runs is still open. Accepted risk: Two close roles can both pass while playing almost alike, and the batches are the largest part of the check load. Core.
    • One effective-ability layer holds four families of temporary state: body (in-match energy, carried fitness, match sharpness, minor knocks), mind (morale, confidence, form), familiarity (the position rating and the player’s grasp of the tactic), and surroundings (crowd, pitch, weather). Each family has its own cap, the caps add, and body and mind have the largest, so an 18 has poor patches and hot streaks but never becomes a poor player and a hot player never becomes unstoppable. Partnerships and cohesion sit in the team layer, not here. Core.
    • Every overlapping group of attributes has one split. Vision sets which passing options a player sees, decisions how well the player picks among them and when, and anticipation how early the player reads an opponent. Pace is top speed in long runs; acceleration wins short races. Jumping is height and timing, heading is what the player does on contact, and strength holds position in any contact. Aggression is how often and how hard a player commits, with the foul risk; tackling is success once committed. Concentration is rare sudden lapses that grow late in a match; consistency is the hidden everyday spread of each action, within and between matches. Technique shapes how every action is done and which skills a player tries; first touch is control on receipt. Agility is turning and gates hard body skills; balance is staying up and striking off balance. Composure acts on each action under pressure; the pressure trait acts on big occasions. Accepted risk: Lapses are rare, so the sensitivity check needs larger batches to see concentration move. Core.
    • What the board and the fans expect of a club’s style blends club identity with the owner type, and each owner type sets its own weight, for example a state-backed owner weighs its own targets more. Accepted risk: Two inputs to one expectation, with a weight per owner type to tune. Core.
    • Tactic familiarity lives with the player: each player holds a grasp of each tactic. The team value is computed from the players on the pitch and never stored; actions of one player read the player’s grasp, and team actions read the team value. Core.
    • Every rating in the game, for staff, managers, referees, and agents as well as players, uses the 1-to-20 scale stored in tenths. Why: One scale to learn. Accepted risk: The referee’s numbers are sized from published data in the engine’s own units, so each must be converted to and from the 1-to-20 scale. Core.
    • Every staff, manager, and agent rating has a named job, and a season-long sensitivity check runs before each release. Accepted risk: Season-long batches are slow, on top of the match batches at every milestone. Core.
    • The referee’s numbers are hidden: the manager sees a description such as ‘strict with cards’ before the match and learns more from past matches. Core.
    • A relationship between two people carries two numbers: on-pitch understanding, earned from minutes together, and personal liking, moved by personality, groups, and events; two players can combine well and dislike each other. Core.
    • The game stores every pair at every club in the active leagues, and none at the light clubs. Accepted risk: Memory and save size grow with the number of active leagues, on top of the full staff and agent records already chosen for them. Core.
    • Each player keeps every event of recent matches, for replays and deep reports, and match summaries for older ones. Accepted risk: Large saves, which adds to the load on the player’s machine that session six already named as the sharpest risk. Core.
    • Both numbers of a relationship fade when two people are apart, at different speeds. Core.
    • Height sets a player’s standing reach in the air, jumping adds to it, and heading decides what happens on contact; tall players also turn a little slower, so height is a trade-off. Accepted risk: Agility and height both touch turning, so the sensitivity test must separate them. Core.
    • Build is a description such as slight, athletic, or powerful, generated from the attributes, with no effect in the engine; weight is not stored. Accepted risk: Build is a field with no engine job, the kind your brief warns against, although it is shown as a word and derived from attributes that do have jobs. Core.
    • Besides the age curves, age acts directly: older players recover more slowly between matches, carry more injury risk under congestion, and fade faster late in a match. Accepted risk: Age overlaps stamina, natural fitness, and injury proneness, so the sensitivity test must separate them. Core.
    • A player new to a country adapts over months: language, climate, and the league’s style move the mind family of effective ability, beside the off-pitch effects of nationality. Accepted risk: One more state to track for each player. Core.
    • When a manager wins against the club’s identity, three reactions mix, and each club sets their strength: results buy time, supporter groups split between results and style, and at clubs with a strong identity a winning manager can still lose standing over time. Accepted risk: At some clubs a manager can be sacked while winning, which some players of the genre find unfair. Core.
    • Where no real history exists, identity is computed from the simulated past seasons the save generates: how the club played and won. Core.
    • On the pitch a club’s style comes from two layers, the squad and the tactic. Club identity shapes both from outside by two routes: it shapes who the club hires and buys, and the board and fans expect it, so a manager who plays against it loses standing and the AI manager weighs it in its philosophy. Accepted risk: Two slow loops to tune, and style on the pitch no longer carries a separate identity input for the realism test to check. This replaced: a style of three layers, identity, squad, and tactic, each adding its own part on the pitch. Core.
    • A club’s identity at the start of a save is seeded from the real club’s history and reputation, with a fallback where no source exists. Accepted risk: It needs a data source for the clubs of every active league. Core.
    • Identity drifts slowly towards how the club actually plays when it wins, and jumps at big events such as a takeover, a legendary manager, relegation, or a trophy. Accepted risk: The most tuning of the three ways identity can change. Core.
    • The player layer keeps what is personal, the position rating and the player’s grasp of the tactic; partnerships and cohesion move to the team layer and act only on actions that need two or more players. Why: Each quality then counts once. Core.
    • Club identity acts only off the pitch; it reaches play only through what the club does off it. Core.
    • Team numbers act on actions that need several players at once: pressing chains, the offside line, and combinations between pairs. Why: A team can be more than its players, for example in coordinated pressing. Accepted risk: A second place to tune beside the player layer. Core.
    • Each kind of wider data gets its own data rules, decided as the kind is examined, rather than the player’s rules applied unchanged. Accepted risk: Slower, and the kinds may drift apart in scale and in how they are checked. Core.
    • The data rules of the player (a job for every field, one scale, and a sensitivity check) are examined for four more kinds of data: team-level numbers, the other rated people, relationships and history, and the physical and fixed facts. Core.
    • Every role ships with all three behaviours together: where the player stands in and out of possession, which actions the player prefers, and how teammates treat the player (a target forward draws long balls). Accepted risk: It is the largest piece of work and the most tuning in the data-model area. Core.
    • A duty (defend, support, attack) scales the role’s own behaviour, for example an attacking full-back overlaps more and tracks back less. Accepted risk: Every role needs three tuned variants. Core.
    • The game offers the full genre list of about 40 roles. Accepted risk: With three duties and three behaviours each, about 360 tuned variants, and many roles differ only slightly. Core.
    • Consistency and injury proneness are hidden values, beside the twelve hidden personality traits; injury proneness replaces the visible injury-resistance attribute. Accepted risk: The manager learns them only by watching and through staff, over seasons. Core.
    • For the manager’s own players, the visible attributes are exact once the player signs; hidden values stay hidden. Accepted risk: Scouting matters only for the market, and a signing shows its true visible numbers on the first day. Core.
    • A hidden value shows itself to the manager in words, for example ‘thrives on big occasions’, with a confidence set by the staff, as personality already does. Core.
    • Match sharpness is its own state that rises with minutes in matches and falls without them, and it also feeds carried fitness and form. Core.
    • Each state acts on its own attributes: tiredness hits the physical attributes first, low morale the mental ones, a knock the physical ones, and sharpness the timing of technical actions. Core.
    • The strength of the convex curve is set against the five-match target: a top player looks clearly better than an average player in the same role in about two of three runs of five matches. The factor of two for the top gap is only a starting value. Why: The factor of two was picked, not measured, and the five-match target is already the board’s measure of felt quality. Accepted risk: The target sets the whole curve, not the top gap alone, so the top gap can end at any ratio the target allows. This replaced: a fixed rule that the gap from 18 to 20 counts twice the gap from 10 to 12. Core.
    • The engine stores attributes in tenths from 1.0 to 20.0, and the screen shows whole numbers from 1 to 20. Why: Development and ageing move smoothly, while the screen keeps the genre’s familiar scale. Accepted risk: Two players shown as 15 can differ slightly in play. This replaced: whole numbers from 1 to 20 in storage as well as on screen. Core.
    • A player’s overall level follows a normal curve centred on 10 with a spread of about 3.5 on the 1-to-20 scale, wider than the research suggestion of 2.8; single attributes spread wider still, each tier has its own mean, and the largest drop is from the first tier to the second. Why: Tiers overlap more, so a lower-league gem is common. Accepted risk: The world shows more upsets than real football, which presses on the cup upset band and the per-match realism bands. Core.
    • The curve that makes the top of the scale matter more acts in both places: on each attribute before the blend, and on the blended result of each stage. Core.
    • Hard skills are gated three ways together: each skill has a technique threshold and an agility threshold; ball skills such as flicks, volleys, and chips lean on technique while body skills such as overhead kicks, diving headers, and slide tackles lean on agility; and technique decides whether the player tries the skill while agility decides whether the body pulls it off. Core.
    • The blend mixes three rules: an action runs in stages (see, choose, execute, under pressure), each stage has one main attribute with two or three smaller supporting weights, and the weights are fitted from real data where it exists and set by design where it does not. Why: Stages give each mental attribute its own moment, a main attribute keeps the rule readable on screen, and fitted weights keep it honest. Accepted risk: Several stages per action add calculations on every tick, which presses on the engine’s CPU tripwire that the other realism additions already strain. Core.
    • Attributes intermingle in their effects: a calculation reads several attributes together, not one attribute per calculation. Why: Football actions draw on several qualities at once, and a blend stops any single attribute carrying an outcome alone. Core.
    • Pace is kept from dominating three ways together: it acts mainly in long runs while acceleration decides short races, every sprint costs stamina so a fast player who runs often fades late, and the sensitivity test fails pace when its effect is out of proportion. Why: Real top speed is rare, 0.7 percent of live time above 7 metres per second. Core.

    Action resolution and the probability layer

    • Every player carries every attribute. Keepers use the outfield attributes wherever they fit: the dive reads agility, the stance reads positioning, tackling joins rushing out, and keepers can dribble, score, and show flair. Every outfield player carries hidden goalkeeper attributes, generally poor, set at creation and never developed. Accepted risk: An agile outfield player in goal dives like a keeper, limited only by the hidden poor keeper attributes. Core.
    • The error sources stay separate and split the error band: the tails of the contact draws, concentration lapses, and the named error events each get a fixed share of the errors leading to goals, each is tuned to its share, and the report counts each source apart. Accepted risk: Three shares to set and tune, and a named event must never also appear as a tail draw of the same kind. Core.
    • The season variance bands (the home, draw, and away split, the share of points spread explained by quality, top-quarter against bottom-half results, and Poisson goals) each carry the real target and a design target with a little more upset; the test passes on the design target and reports the distance from real football. Why: The wide world spread of ability was chosen on purpose and gives more upsets than real football. Core.
    • Team strength is measured two ways for the season check: a strength from the ratings of each club’s best eleven must predict points, and an Elo-style rating built from the simulated results must match real Elo spreads between clubs and tiers. Core.
    • The realism test bands errors on Opta’s definition, with a wide band: errors leading to goals at 0.25 to 0.45 a match (8 to 15 percent of goals), keepers about a quarter of them, and a team spread that depends on quality and composure. It adds a check on high turnovers: 11 to 17 a match, 15 to 17 percent ending in a shot. Why: It is the public, per-team measure fans see, and the season-to-season swing is large. Accepted risk: High turnovers mix pressing wins with errors, so a failure there may point at the pressing model rather than at errors. Core.
    • Errors come from three sources: the far tails of every contact draw, whose spread widens with pressure, fatigue, low composure, and low consistency; rare concentration lapses that grow late in a match; and a small set of named error events (a short back pass, a keeper spilling a cross, a slip), each with its own chance, so commentary and reports can name them. Accepted risk: A second source of errors to calibrate beside the tails of the draws, and the two can double-count. Core.
    • A duel runs in two steps: physics decides the approach, the angle and closing speed from pace, acceleration, and positions; a first draw decides whether the defender commits or holds off, from decisions and aggression; then the contest draw reads the attacker’s dribbling, agility, acceleration, balance, and strength against the defender’s tackling, positioning, anticipation, and aggression, with the angle, the closing speed, and the local numbers. Accepted risk: Two draws per duel, and duels are the most common contest in a match. Core.
    • A duel ends in one of four outcomes: the attacker keeps the ball, the defender wins it clean, the ball runs loose, or a foul. Why: When a defender wins, possession changes only about half the time, so loose balls are a real phase. Core.
    • The duel model is checked by win rate by local numbers, the foul share of duels (about 11 percent), dribble success by third, and the turnover split after each winner. Core.
    • A pass is a contact whose draw adds an error to direction, weight, and height, from passing and technique, the pass type, pressure on the passer, the weaker foot, and body shape; vision and decisions act earlier, in the choice. When a defender in the lane can reach the ball, a draw from anticipation and positioning decides whether the defender cuts it out. The receiver’s run sets where the ball is met, and receiving is its own first-touch draw from first touch, ball speed and height, and pressure at receiving. Core.
    • The pass model is checked by completion by pass kind, by length, and by pressure at receiving, and by how often players choose the safe open receiver compared with real players. Core.
    • A keeper’s save mixes physics and draws: the keeper’s position and dive decide whether the ball can be reached; only then a draw decides hold, parry, or fumble from the shot’s speed and closeness and the keeper’s handling, with composure under pressure; a small third draw sets a parry’s direction, weighted away from goal by a good keeper. One-on-ones set how the keeper narrows the angle; rushing out and command of area act on crosses and through balls. Core.
    • A strike takes two draws: first a quality (clean, scuff, slice), which pressure or a bad touch can force, then the error in aim, height, and power inside that quality, from finishing, technique, composure, pressure, body part, and the weaker foot. Accepted risk: Two draws per shot to calibrate, and more work on each tick with a shot. Core.
    • A penalty mixes a guessing game with an ordinary strike: the taker picks a side, the keeper guesses with habits the other side can learn, and the strike’s error and the keeper’s reach then apply as for any shot. Core.
    • Three checks guard against shots that are too accurate: the xG-bucket check, a band on the split of wide, blocked, and on-target shots, and conversion by distance. Core.
    • A probability is drawn at each contact, the moment a player touches the ball or the ball meets a player: the tackle, the first touch, the keeper’s handling, and the quality of a strike or a pass. Each draw comes from a chance or a spread built from the situation and the attributes. Between contacts, flight, deflections, bodies in the lane, and the keeper’s position are physics. Why: Real data measures outcomes at contacts, so they calibrate directly, and physics between them keeps blocks and interceptions emergent; the xG buckets then check the whole chain. Accepted risk: Two systems to tune, and a bad xG bucket can come from either one. Core.
    • The xG-bucket check judges the engine with an xG model fitted on public real shots, reading distance, angle, body part, shot type, and pressure, independent of the engine. Why: The engine must not mark its own homework. Accepted risk: The event contract and the test-only output must carry body part, shot type, and pressure for every shot. Core.

    Machine load and save size

    • No realism lever is pulled to meet the budget: background matches keep the full engine, the active leagues stay as the player chooses, people and pairs stay full records, and every match keeps its events. Accepted risk: On an ordinary laptop the budget is very likely to fail, since today’s engine already takes about 20 seconds per league season on eight cores and this session multiplied the work per tick and the data per save.
    • When the budget fails, engineering gives way first: the team optimises (parallel matches, compact storage, cheaper maths) until the budget holds, and returns to you only if it still fails; no realism is cut before then. Accepted risk: An open-ended amount of optimisation work.
    • The game promises to run well on an ordinary laptop, about 4 cores and 8 GB of memory. Why: The widest audience. Accepted risk: Tight limits on matchday time, save creation, save size, and memory, against a world of full people, every pair, and full recent events.
    • The budget has two forms: fixed limits for what the player feels (matchday wait, save creation time, save size, memory) on the reference laptop, and a relative limit per match against the shipped engine as an early warning in the tests.

    Realism calibration and validation

    • Each engine change runs a quick set of about 1,000 matches automatically, which judges goals and most per-match rates; rare-event bands, scorelines, and the table wait for the heavier runs. Every report is stored with its version and compared with the last, and a band that moves by more than its tolerance is flagged even when it still passes. Accepted risk: About 7 minutes of 8 cores per change at today’s match cost, and the repository has no automated job to run it yet. Core.
    • Every league is banded, in three layers: each league with public data gets its own targets from five recent seasons, each tier gets a target set, and the other leagues fall into banding groups by tier and strength whose bands come from a formula fitted on the leagues with data. Shots count blocked shots, and pass completion and errors follow Opta’s count. Accepted risk: The formula extrapolates, so a league unlike any measured one can get a wrong band and send tuning the wrong way. Core.
    • football-data.co.uk and FotMob’s Opta pass figures are used for measuring only, under the same accepted risk as StatsBomb and Transfermarkt: the game ships none of the data, and each band records its source so it can be measured again elsewhere. Accepted risk: FotMob serves Opta data for display in its own app, not for reuse, so a complaint means measuring the pass bands again from open sets before release. Core.
    • Each band’s tolerance is the real spread of the statistic across leagues and seasons, widened by the sampling noise of the batch size, so a small quick run does not fail by chance while a full run is judged tightly. Core.
    • The distribution check compares the share of every scoreline (0-0, 1-0, 1-1, up to 4-3 and beyond) with real shares, and compares the goals, shots, and cards totals as whole distributions. Core.
    • The season check bands the league table in full, per league, with real and design targets: champion points, the points of the relegated places, the spread of points per game, and the title margin; both the full engine and the fast model must pass. Core.
    • A division’s style emerges from its players and their tactics, with nothing set per division by hand; the bands check the result per division. Core.

    Tactical counterplay and team-level AI

    • Two guards stop a human exploit of the AI: an AI manager notices during the match what is hurting it, for example repeated balls in behind, and answers with the counter the round-robin shows, within its fingerprint and adaptability; and before each release an automated exploit hunt plays many tactics against the AI and fails the build if any tactic wins far above its expected rate. Accepted risk: The exploit hunt adds a large search to each release run, and in-match learning adds AI work during the match. Core.
    • Players manage a game by default from the score, the clock, and their personality (wasting time, tactical fouls, dropping deep), and the manager’s instructions push it up or down, each with its cost in cards and pressure. Core.
    • The manager has a small set of touchline shouts (encourage, demand more, calm down, focus) that move the mind family of effective ability for a while, with a cost when overused. Core.
    • Conditions change which tactic works, and it emerges: a press in heat drains energy faster, a poor pitch worsens short passes and touches, and wind moves long balls; the round-robin runs under several conditions to prove it. Accepted risk: The round-robin grows by the number of conditions it runs under. Core.
    • The fit check builds, for each named style, a suitable squad and an unsuitable one of the same overall level (for example fast, fit pressers against slow ones for the high press), plays both against the same opponents, and requires a clear gap in points and in the style’s own statistics. Core.
    • An AI manager judges a tactic two ways: before each match it reads the same noisy staff advice the human gets (fit estimates and the analysts’ opposition report), weighed by its philosophy and attributes; over seasons it remembers which tactics worked for it and against whom, and shifts towards them. Core.
    • Each AI manager has a tactical fingerprint, a recognisable preferred style and habits such as switching to a back five when leading late; analysts reveal it, and a hidden adaptability sets how often the manager breaks habit. Core.
    • The AI check requires AI managers to pick tactics that suit their squads more often than not, and AI-run clubs to reproduce the measured possession rule over a season. Core.
    • The round-robin plays about eight named styles (possession, low block, high press, counter-attack, direct, wing play, gegenpress, park the bus) with identical squads, every pairing home and away, on every change, and a grid over the settings themselves (line height, press, tempo, width, directness) before each release. Accepted risk: The setting grid adds many pairings to each release run. Core.
    • A tactic dominates, and the check fails, when a preset loses clearly to no other preset or takes more than a set share of all points in the round-robin; every preset must have at least one clear weakness. Core.
    • Rest defence has no setting of its own: the players held back while the team attacks follow from the mentality and each player’s duty. Core.
    • The gaps between the lines and the team’s width emerge from where each role stands, moved by the block law; no instruction sets a target gap. Accepted risk: An instruction may change the gaps too little to notice, which only the tactic sensitivity check will catch. Core.
    • Costs and counters emerge from play, with no counter table: a high line is exposed because a slow defender loses the race to a ball in behind, and a press tires players because pressing costs energy; the round-robin check proves the counters. Accepted risk: If a dominant tactic emerges, only a change to a mechanism can fix it, which is slower than editing a table, and a cost can come out too small to notice. Core.
    • Squad fit has no number of its own: each player carries out an instruction with their own attributes and state, so a slow, tired squad presses badly on its own; the fit check proves it. Core.

    Match engine structure

    • Each player and each kind of action draws from its own random stream, so a change to one draw does not reshuffle the rest of the match, and paired seeds work for every sensitivity check. Replay stays identical on every machine. Accepted risk: The engine’s random layer changes, and every recorded replay and baseline is regenerated once. Core.
    • A skipped match may give slightly different odds than a watched one; the game accepts that gap, and the equality check measures it from sampled mid-match states and reports it. Accepted risk: A result can depend a little on whether the player watched, which your brief ruled out. Core.
    • When the player skips to the result mid-match, the fast model finishes the match from the current state: the score, the clock, the cards, and the fatigue. Accepted risk: The fast model must match the full engine from any match state, not only from kick-off, or a result will depend on whether the player watched. Core.
    • All background matches of a matchday run as fast as the machine allows, before or during the player’s match, and their events are revealed to the player at the moment each one happens on the player’s match clock. Core.
    • A mid-match save made before a game update finishes the match on the engine version it started with, and the season then continues on the new version. Accepted risk: Each release must ship the previous engine version beside the new one, for as long as unfinished matches from it may exist. Core.
    • The engine has a written state machine for open play, each dead-ball kind, set pieces, stoppages, half-time, and full-time, with entry and exit conditions, and a check that every player stands in a legal, sensible place at each restart, for example the opponents outside the box at a goal kick and the shape restored before play resumes.
    • Each ground has its own length and width within the laws, real grounds take their real size where known, and the size emerges in play, for example a narrow pitch helps a low block. Accepted risk: The bands must hold across pitch sizes, and a ground far from 105 by 68 can move a club’s statistics away from its league’s band.
    • Added time keeps pricing each kind of event at a set number of seconds, with video reviews added as a kind, a small random variance, and the cap.
    • A match replays identically on every supported machine and operating system: the engine avoids maths that differs between machines, and a test replays a recorded match on each supported system. Accepted risk: Some fast maths is off limits, which works against the laptop budget.
    • The fast model must pass the same season bands as the full engine, and on a fixed fixture batch it must match the full engine per pairing of team strengths: win, draw, and loss shares and goals within the same tolerance. Why: A strong team must not do better in one model while the league totals still pass.
    • The fast model’s numbers are fitted from full-engine results after each engine change, so it follows the engine automatically, and the equality check runs with the harness. Accepted risk: Each engine change needs enough full-engine matches to refit the fast model, which adds to the harness run.

    Player decision-making and off-ball movement

    • A positional instruction, such as stay wide or hold the line, is a limit on where the player may go, and a habit can break it only under pressure or low mood. A choice instruction, such as shoot less or play direct, moves the scores of the options it names, and the player’s decisions and teamwork set how faithfully the player follows it. The rule that an instruction is carried out as given now covers the limits only. Accepted risk: A player can drift from a choice instruction, which a manager may read as the game ignoring the instruction unless the match report names it. Core.
    • The keeper uses the same picture, utility, and claims as outfield players, with keeper options (come for a cross or stay, sweep or hold, throw, roll, short pass, long kick, punt) and the keeper’s attributes. The measured depth rule is the keeper’s anchor, organisation of the wall and the line is calls from communication and command of area, and the keeper’s choices are checked against the keeper bands. Accepted risk: Keeper organisation has no public data, so only watching checks it. Core.
    • When the ball changes hands, the team’s counter-press instruction sets a window and how aggressive it is, and inside the window each player’s defensive utility, with claims and calls, picks counter-press or recover goal-side from distance to the ball, team-mates nearby, the danger behind, and the player’s work rate and energy. Core.
    • Players near the ball or in a duel think on every tick; the others think at a human rate set by decisions and anticipation, about every 0.1 to 0.3 seconds, and any new event (a pass, a touch, a call) wakes a player at once. Movement still updates on every tick. Accepted risk: A player far from the ball reacts a little later than one near it, and the think rate must never change the result of a replay. Core.
    • Four band families join the realism test: shape (width and depth by phase, the three line heights by the ball’s distance from goal, the gaps between lines, and players goal-side of the ball); crowding (players within 5, 10, and 15 metres of the ball, even between the teams, and the share of frames with at most one player near the ball); behaviour (the run mix by kind, time on the ball and losses by pressure, defender handoffs, the goal-side share, and PPDA); and a new frozen-player band on how long an outfielder stays still or out of the play, measured from tracking. Accepted risk: Most of these come from 7 DFL and 20 SkillCorner matches, and the frozen-player measure does not exist yet. Core.
    • Two bands guard against passes through crowds beside the interception draw: pass completion by the number of opponents near the lane, and how often players choose a pass whose lane holds opponents, against the real choice model. Accepted risk: The lane-crowding measure must be computed from tracking first, and the watched review does not name it as a separate item. Core.
    • Attackers share space and time their runs three ways together: the role patterns carry the known pairings, such as the winger going wide when the full-back underlaps; for the free choices a runner claims a space or a run and team-mates lower their score for it, and runners call for the ball; and a space a team-mate holds or is running into is worth less. Off-ball movement and anticipation time a run against the offside line. Core.
    • Defenders share jobs by claims: each scores the jobs alone, and a player who claims a job makes team-mates lower their score for it, so two players rarely press the same attacker. Players also call to each other, such as ‘I’ve got him’ and ‘push up’, and communication and leadership decide how well calls land, so a quiet back line coordinates worse. Accepted risk: Coordination emerges rather than being assigned, so the no-clustering bands and the watched review must catch a back line that fails to organise. Core.
    • A player’s scan rate comes from vision and anticipation, falling under close pressure and near the opponent’s goal, and the player chooses when each scan happens: good players scan just before receiving, and a scan while the ball is arriving risks the first touch. Accepted risk: A scan-timing choice for every player on every tick, and the scan rate has only one public source to band against. Core.
    • A trait can bias a choice, such as shooting from distance, and set a signature way of doing an action, such as cutting inside onto the stronger foot or chipping the keeper one-on-one. Match reports count how often each player used each trait, so the manager and the analysts can spot it. Core.
    • The option list is the whole catalogue of 53 actions, each family with its own measured outcome curve, plus the actions a manager game needs that no provider labels, such as time-wasting at the corner flag, a deliberate foul to stop a counter, and a quick free kick. Accepted risk: The largest option set in the design, each action with a contact draw and a band, and the unlabelled extras are checked only by watching. Core.
    • A poor decision-maker goes wrong two ways together: the player’s estimate of each option’s chance and value is noisy, with the noise shrinking as decisions rises, and the pick is a soft choice weighted towards the best score, so a poor decision-maker often takes the second or third option. Composure widens both under pressure. Core.
    • An option’s score is expected value: the chance of success times the value gained, minus the chance of losing the ball times the danger of losing it there. Its weights and the risk appetite are fitted so that the engine’s choices match the real choice model, which weighs completion, a running receiver, and a forward option far above threat. Instruction, role, and personality move the risk appetite. Core.
    • The carrier’s options grow beyond shoot, pass to feet, dribble, clear, and hold: passes into space (a lead pass and a through ball onto a run); several carry paths scored apart; crosses (driven, floated, cut-back) and switches of play; and body options (turn, shield, hold up the ball, draw a foul, a one-two, and skill moves gated by technique and agility). More options are wanted beyond these. Accepted risk: Many more options to score for the carrier on every tick, each with its own contact draw to calibrate. Core.
    • A player’s picture holds where each seen player stood and where that player was heading, so a passer can lead a runner seen during the run and a defender can read a run early. Team-mates also call: a runner calls for the ball and a team-mate warns ‘man on’, and a call reaches a player without a scan. Communication sets how often and how well a player calls, which gives that attribute a job in the match. Accepted risk: Calls add a message layer between players, and a call that arrives late or is wrong must be modelled too. Core.
    • The team’s marking system (zonal, man-to-man, or a mix) is a tactical setting that sets each defender’s default, and a defensive utility picks each player’s job within it on every tick: press, cover, track a runner, mark, or hold the line. Accepted risk: A second utility for every player without the ball, and the system must still reproduce the measured handoffs instead of long man-marking. Core.
    • Off-ball movement has three layers: an anchor from the shape and the role; role patterns for the named runs, each with a trigger; and an off-ball utility that picks among the runs and the anchor and adds support angles and space seeking. Accepted risk: The largest new piece of work in the area, and a new per-tick load for every player without the ball. Core.
    • Pressure acts twice. Before the choice, a player needs a decision time set by decisions and first touch; after it, a set time to carry the action out, for example setting the body for a long pass. A presser who arrives inside either time hurries the player into a wider error, which composure narrows, or blocks the action. Accepted risk: Two timers per player with the ball, and many more held or blocked actions to calibrate against the measured losses under pressure. Core.
    • Each player keeps a picture of the pitch that scans refresh. Vision and anticipation set how often a player scans and how wide each scan reaches; a player out of view is remembered where the player was last seen, so a stale picture causes real mistakes such as a pass to a team-mate who has moved. Accepted risk: Twenty-two pictures to keep and update on every tick, and replay must stay identical on every machine. Core.

    Output and presentation

    • English ships at launch, and the commentary banks and the glossary are built so that other languages can follow. Core.
    • Each screen (live match, half-time, post-match report, data hub, opposition analysis, player profile) ships with a designed set from the catalogue’s map; the player can add, remove, and arrange panels; and the analysts push the panels that matter in this match, such as the pressing map when the press is failing. Core.
    • Each metric has one game definition on every main screen, written in the glossary with the provider it follows; the analyst view in the data hub can switch to a named provider’s variant. Core.
    • The game shows an xG fitted on simulated outcomes from the features a real provider sees (location, angle, body part, pressure, assist type). The engine’s true scoring chance stays inside the engine for calibration, so xG keeps the puzzle of luck against skill and never reveals finishing or keeping skill. Core.
    • Four aids help a player read the figures: a plain name and a one-line definition on every metric; a simple view with a few key figures and an analyst view with the full catalogue; staff sentences that turn figures into words and link back to them; and a good-or-bad mark on each figure against the league, the season, or the opponent. Core.
    • Every statistic and visualisation is true for everyone. Depth comes from staff: better analysts unlock more visualisations and faster, deeper analysis. The human manager’s tactical attribute acts only on readings and estimates, such as staff reports, opposition reports, and scouting. Core.
    • Every full-engine match keeps its decision internals (what each carrier saw, the options scored, and the true causes) while it is recent, so opposition analysis can replay an opponent’s decisions as well as the manager’s own. Accepted risk: The largest addition to save size on the board, on top of every event of recent matches. Core.
    • The fast model produces per-player statistics and ratings drawn from the full engine’s distributions for a player of that quality and role, and a check requires them to match those distributions, so scouting, form, and the market work in every league. Scouting and the market read more than five matches. Accepted risk: The fast model’s per-player output is a second thing to fit and check beside its results. Core.
    • The playtest passes when testers name the main cause on matches with a planted cause and on ordinary matches, scored by two people against the true-cause log, and when testers’ predictions of what a named change would do match a replay of the match with that change. Core.
    • Commentary draws on four kinds of context: player history (scoring runs, milestones, debuts, former clubs, returns from injury); the season and rivals (the table, a title race or relegation fight, a derby, the stakes); threads inside the match (a running duel, a keeper’s great day, a team pinned back, a change that worked); and the true-cause log, so a line can say why. Core.
    • Staff analysis comes at four times: live alerts when an issue persists, with the moments that show it; a half-time talk of the top one to three issues, each with evidence and a suggested change; a post-match account of the result as ranked causes linked to their moments and visualisations; and trends across matches, such as conceding from crosses in four of five matches. Core.
    • Every figure, visualisation, commentary line, rating, and staff sentence is computed from the one match record the viewer replays; a test recomputes every figure from the replayed record and fails on any mismatch; and every figure and staff claim traces back to its moments, so tapping ‘overloaded on the left’ shows the clips and the visualisation that prove it. Core.
    • The game offers a wide set of visualisations: the brief’s set (shot maps with xG, an xG timeline, pass networks, heat maps, average positions, and ratings); tactical ones (overload and zone-control maps by phase, chances by lane, pressing and regain maps, shape by phase with line heights); player ones (action maps, sprint and distance figures, trait use); comparisons against the opponent, the season, and the last match; a momentum or threat-flow line and possession chains; set-piece and keeper maps; energy curves and duel maps; and moment replays with overlays of pitch control, the options the carrier saw and missed, and the line heights. Visualisations update as the match runs, and a full catalogue of statistics, model metrics, and visualisations, with a home for each on the match screen, the data hub, the post-match report, and opposition analysis, is being researched. Accepted risk: A very large surface to design and keep true, and pass networks wait for passer and receiver on every pass from the event-contract redesign. Core.
    • Each moment gets a highlight score from three kinds: how far it moved, or could have moved, the win probability; whether the manager may act on it (a tiring player, a card, a change of shape, an overload building); and whether it tells a story (a first goal, a milestone, a rival’s red card, a named error from the cause log). Each highlight level includes more of each kind, and the manager picks the level. Core.
    • A player’s match rating is built in three steps: each action earns the change it makes to the chance of scoring and of conceding, tackles, blocks, and pressures that force a loss included; off-ball work earns credit from the true-cause log, such as a run that pulled a defender and opened space, a cover position that closed a lane, or a press that forced a long ball; and the total is compared with what the player’s role and the match allowed, so a centre-back in a dominant team is not punished for having little to do. The result shows on the familiar 1-to-10 display. Accepted risk: The ratings will differ from the public sites that players know, which reward attackers, so a player may read a fair rating as a strange one. Core.
    • Commentary comes from large banks of lines, each tagged with the context it fits and chosen with cooldowns. A language model writes and tags most of the banks before release and a person reviews them; a small grammar assembles some sentences from parts for extra variety. No model runs during play, and every fact in a line comes from the event record, never from the line. Accepted risk: Reviewing many thousands of generated lines is a large editorial task. Core.
    • The game ships written commentary with crowd, whistle, and ball sounds that follow the events, such as the crowd rising on a chance; there is no spoken voice. Accepted risk: A commercial audio set is needed, and the sounds must follow the event record as exactly as the text does. Core.
    • Explanations hold three layers: every statistic and picture is always true, so a player who doubts the assistant can check the evidence; the staff’s reading of what happened can miss or misweigh causes as staff quality falls; and advice about what to do next can be wrong as all staff advice can. The playtest uses an average staff. Accepted risk: A poor assistant can still mislead a player who does not check the evidence. Core.
    • The engine writes a log of true causes as the match runs, for example that a goal came from a three-against-two on the left after the full-back pushed up. The staff read that log through their own quality, and the same log scores the playtest by comparing a playtester’s answer with the true causes. Accepted risk: The engine must decide what counts as a cause, which is itself a model that can be wrong. Core.

    In-game management

    • After a skip, the manager’s prepared game-state plans and triggers apply automatically for the rest of the match, and the fast model reflects them. Core.
    • Every in-match tool is open to the AI manager, and its tactical attributes and adaptability set how many it uses and how well: a weak manager makes fewer, cruder changes, while a strong one uses formations, roles, opposition targets, player instructions, plans, substitutions, and shouts. Core.
    • The check covers every in-match tool and level, each with an expected direction and a cost, so no control does nothing; a milestone checks the tools its changes could affect, and a release checks all of them. This extends the tactic sensitivity check into the match. Core.
    • The in-match change check plays matches to a set state, for example 0-1 at minute 60, then forks each one with paired seeds: one copy makes the change and one does not. The differences in shots, xG, and goals for and against are banded against real game-state data, such as trailing teams taking about 3.4 points more of the shots and a formation change lifting attacking output over the next ten minutes. Accepted risk: Forked matches need saved mid-match states and split random streams, and the real effect sizes come from few studies. Core.
    • The manager has four kinds of in-match tools: team instructions (line height, pressing, width, tempo, directness, and mentality); opposition targets (press a weak player, attack his side, show him onto his weaker foot, and close down or mark a dangerous one); player instructions (stay wide, get forward, hold position, take fewer risks, shoot on sight, man-mark a named opponent); and prepared game-state plans switched with one action, such as protect a lead or chase the game. Accepted risk: Many more controls, each one a candidate for an exploit, which the exploit hunt must cover. Core.
    • During the match the manager also sees: each player’s state (energy, sharpness, a knock, card risk, morale, and whether the player has taken up the last instruction); the analysts’ read of the opponent (shape, changes, and who is tiring or struggling, at the analysts’ accuracy); a short read after each change of what it did, such as winning the ball back higher but conceding balls in behind; and the assistant’s one or two proposed changes with their expected cost, which the manager can accept with one action. Core.
    • The medical staff assess every head knock by their quality and advise; the manager makes the call. A confirmed concussion uses a concussion substitute where the competition allows one. Leaving a player on against advice risks a worse injury, a fine, and trouble with the board and the press. Core.
    • Each competition carries its own rule pack from real data: its substitution limit and windows, whether it has concussion substitutes and an extra-time substitute, and which newer laws it applies, such as the keeper’s 8-second rule. Each rule records the date it began, so the simulated past seasons use the rules of their time, for example three substitutes before 2020. Accepted risk: A rule history per competition to research and keep, and the realism bands of past seasons must allow for the older rules. Core.
    • The size of a change’s effect emerges from behaviour and is kept in bounds three ways: the batch check bands each change’s effect against real game-state data; the release exploit hunt also tries sequences of changes during a match and fails any sequence that wins far above its expected rate; and every change carries a real cost, for example more attacking means more exposure behind and faster tiring, so no change is free. Core.
    • The AI manager shares three things with the human: the same rules (substitution limits and windows, gates, and shouts, with no action open to only one side); the same information (only what its staff can show it, through the same noisy readings, never the engine’s true state); and the same reaction time (it notices a problem after its staff do and cannot change on the tick a threat appears). Accepted risk: An AI limited to staff readings and human reaction plays worse, and the exploit hunt must still find no easy win against it. Core.
    • The player can pause at any moment, at several speeds, and queue changes while paused, and can also choose automatic pauses, for example on a goal, an injury, a card, or an assistant alert. Core.
    • Substitutions and formation changes wait for a stoppage. Shouts and instructions, such as push higher, slow down, or target the left-back, are called from the touchline and start at once, and players take them up over time: players near the touchline and those with high decisions, teamwork, and tactic familiarity adopt a change first, and the captain’s calls spread it. Accepted risk: A change reaches players at different times, so the team can play two ideas at once for a while, which the effect-size check must allow for. Core.

    Set pieces

    • The best set-piece coach and routines together can reach the top of the real spread, about 0.065 xG and about 6 percent goals per corner, and never beyond it; the per-team spread of set-piece xG is itself a band, so the world’s teams spread like real ones. Core.
    • Penalty rate and conversion are banded per league, with the rate raised where the competition uses video review, and shoot-out conversion is banded apart. A shoot-out pressure curve lowers conversion on kicks that decide elimination, through composure and the pressure trait, checked against the real 70 and 44 percent. Core.
    • Each league has a set-piece goal share band from recent seasons, about 20 to 28 percent without penalties and a quarter to a third with them. The world’s set-piece style may drift as AI managers copy what works, for example a long-throw boom spreading, while the season check holds the share inside a wider range. Accepted risk: A drifting world can move a league away from its real band, so the season check must tell a drift from a fault. Core.
    • The taker decides whether to take a restart quickly, by the taker’s decisions and the team instruction; a quick restart can go wrong or be called back. Defenders can stand in the way or delay it, at the risk of a card. Core.
    • Each routine has a familiarity that drilling raises and time fades, and an unfamiliar routine is carried out with wider timing and position errors. A squad holds only a limited number of routines well at once, set by the coaches and the players’ decisions and teamwork, so more routines make each one weaker. Core.
    • Throw-ins come in three kinds: long-throw routines into the box by a player with the long-throw skill, played like a corner with first contact and second balls; retention routines in the own half with runs that make space; and quick throws before the opponent is set, chosen by the thrower’s decisions and the instruction, at the risk of a foul throw or a poor choice. Core.
    • The manager names takers for each restart kind and side (left and right corners, near and far free kicks, penalties, long throws), each with a fallback order, and the taker picks among the routine’s options in play by what the taker sees, for example going short when the target is double-marked. Core.
    • Three guards stop a runaway routine beside the exploit hunt: each routine’s goal and shot rates are banded against real rates for its kind, and the hunt plays every routine against every defensive setup; AI opponents learn a manager’s favourite routines across matches through their analysts and set their defence against them; and every run, block, and delivery carries the players’ error, so a routine perfect on paper often fails in play. Core.
    • At a set piece, the delivery’s arc and pace come from the taker’s draw; each player’s run, timing, height, and jumping set who can reach the ball; a heading duel draw decides between the players who can reach it; and the keeper chooses to come or stay through the same perception and utility as any player. Legal and illegal contact before the ball arrives, such as blocks, screens, and holding, also happens, and the referee may penalise it. Accepted risk: Contact in the box is a new source of fouls and penalties to calibrate, and the referee must see it through the same model as any foul. Core.
    • The defending manager names the jobs: who marks whom, who guards each post, who stands in the wall, and who stays up for the counter, within a zonal, man-marking, or mixed system. Defenders carry out their jobs with their own attributes and react to the routine they see: a marker follows a decoy run or passes it on, as decisions and communication allow. Core.
    • The manager builds routines three ways: a library of named routines for each restart kind with the taker and target players set; an editor that places each player’s start spot, run, and timing and the delivery target, as on a board; and the set-piece coach’s proposals, which suit the squad and exploit the next opponent using the analysts’ read of how that opponent defends. Accepted risk: A free editor is the easiest place to find an exploit, so the per-routine bands and the exploit hunt must cover edited routines too. Core.

    Fatigue, injuries, and fixture congestion

    • A save keeps daily load and state for recent weeks, a longer window for players in the active leagues, and a compact injury record (type, site, date, days out) for every player’s whole career. Accepted risk: Per-player state for every player everywhere adds to save size beside every pair and full recent events. Core.
    • The season injury and fatigue bands are judged on full-engine seasons in the release run, and the fast model is checked against those seasons’ distributions; between releases, the engine emulator screens injury and fatigue settings so the release run only confirms. Core.
    • An injury crisis reaches beyond the pitch: the board asks why, and the medical and fitness staff’s standing falls when burden runs above what their quality should give; overplayed players lose morale and complain, while a squad that sees the manager protect players trusts him more; and the press runs injury-crisis stories, with fans blaming the manager or the medical staff. Core.
    • A pre-transfer medical by the buying club’s medical staff reads injury proneness and site history at their accuracy, and the club can walk away, renegotiate the fee, or add injury clauses. The shared market valuation also falls with visible injury history, such as days missed in recent seasons, so injury-prone players are cheaper for everyone. Core.
    • Every player everywhere carries condition, load, sharpness, and injuries. The fast model draws injuries and minutes from the full engine’s distributions, and AI managers in background leagues rotate by the same rules, so the market, form, and scouting see real availability. Core.
    • Fatigue differs by role and time: each role’s movement sets its own drain, so full-backs, wide players, and pressing forwards tire faster; a substitute enters with full reserves after a short warm-up curve; extra time continues the reserves about 10 percent below regulation output and adds clearly to the next days’ load; and players recover a little during in-match breaks, such as half-time, the breaks before and within extra time, and long stoppages. Core.
    • Travel adds to accumulated load by distance; crossing time zones adds a jet-lag state of about a day per zone, worse eastward, that dulls decisions and slows recovery; climate moves in-match drain and injury risk, with heat cutting high-intensity running and cold raising muscle risk; and the club plans travel, such as flying one or two days before by charter or scheduled flight, trading cost against recovery. Core.
    • Every match builds sharpness by its level, first-team matches most, then cups, reserve and under-21 matches, then friendlies, and training slows the loss. A match-simulation training session builds some sharpness at a load and injury cost, so a manager can keep a bench sharp without matches. Irregular minutes carry their own risk: a player thrown in after weeks without matches faces a load spike. Core.
    • An injury reaches the mind three ways: a return after a long injury lowers confidence for a while, by personality, and a good start restores it; the player is cautious in the actions that caused the injury, such as fewer committed tackles after an ankle injury, fading with minutes; and the long-term injured feel isolated and frustrated, which staff, the manager’s contact, and team-mates can ease. Core.
    • The medical staff decide routine care. For serious injuries the manager chooses among the options the staff present, such as surgery or rest, a specialist, or a second opinion, each with its time out, cost, and recurrence risk; the player and the agent also have a say, so a player may seek his own specialist or refuse surgery. Better staff and facilities shorten returns and cut recurrence. Core.
    • Illness such as colds, flu, and stomach bugs happens at real rates by season and can spread through a squad, with a few days out and reduced output on return. Accepted risk: A spreading illness can strip a squad before a key match, which a player may find unfair unless the staff’s warnings and prevention make it manageable. Core.
    • Training shapes fitness at three scales: each day’s session adds load and moves fitness, and a good plan follows a match-day rhythm of recovery, load, and taper; a preseason builds a fitness base that the off-season loses, so a short preseason means a weaker base and more early-season injuries; and the fitness staff design the plan by their quality when the manager delegates, and warn when the manager’s plan overloads the squad. Core.
    • The season check bands injuries four ways: counts and burden (injuries per squad and per 1000 match and training hours, days lost, and players available for each match); types and layoffs (the share of each type, the median days out per type, and the severity mix); recurrence (the re-injury share and its timing after return); and the congestion effect (the rise in risk with 4 days’ rest or fewer and late in the second match of a short cycle, against the real ratios). Core.
    • A player low on the slow reserve cuts low-value running, such as jogging back into shape or drifting runs, to protect sprints, by decisions, work rate, and personality; the gaps this opens in the shape are there for the opponent to use. Core.
    • Each injury leaves a raised risk at its body site, high just after return and fading over months with a small raise that stays, plus a small general raise for any injury after a recent one, also fading. Core.
    • Congestion follows real data: it raises injury risk strongly and lowers output only a little, so the reason to rotate is protecting players, as in real football. Accepted risk: Results barely suffer from congestion, so a manager who ignores rotation pays only through injuries, which the injury bands must make felt. Core.
    • Before a match each player shows an injury-risk word (low, raised, or high) from the staff’s reading, with the staff’s reasons, such as three matches in seven days and a hamstring history, so the manager learns what drives risk. Core.
    • Four more forces push rotation beside load and injury risk: squad players short of minutes lose morale and hold the manager to promised roles; players without minutes lose sharpness, so an unrotated bench is not ready; real calendars with midweek cups, European nights, and travel strain the first eleven; and the sports-science staff flag players at risk before each match and propose a rotation. Core.
    • A minor knock can carry into the next days, and the player can play at reduced output and a higher risk of a real injury or rest a few days. Some players hide a knock by personality, so the staff may miss it and the manager may pick a player who is not fit. Core.
    • A return from injury passes through phases: rehab, training, bench minutes, and full load. Each phase restores sharpness in part, and the manager can skip phases at a recurrence cost, against the medical staff’s warning. Core.
    • Injury counts, types, and days lost follow real data; only the long-term after-effects of severe injuries are softer, as decided earlier. Core.
    • Each exposure event (a tackle, a sprint, a collision, a landing, a change of direction) carries a small injury chance, scaled by fatigue, load, injury proneness, age, previous injury, the pitch, and the weather. Each kind of event leads to its own injury types, such as sprints to hamstring strains, tackles to ankle and knee injuries, and landings to knee injuries, each with its own recovery time and recurrence risk. Training injuries also happen, moved by the training plan’s intensity and the medical and fitness staff. Accepted risk: Many small hazards to fit so that the season totals and the type mix both match real data. Core.
    • Between matches a player has a condition that recovers over about three days, faster with natural fitness, youth, and good staff, and an accumulated load over recent weeks from matches, training, and travel. High load slows recovery, lowers top output, and raises injury risk, so congestion bites even when condition looks full. The manager sees load only through the sports-science staff’s readings, as accurate as their quality. Core.
    • In-match fatigue has three parts: a short-term reserve that sprints use and brief rest refills, which sets repeated-sprint ability; a slow reserve that drains through the match; and mental fatigue that raises concentration lapses and slows decisions late, which personality and the fitness staff move. Late tiredness is real but modest, and bands on running by 15-minute period keep the physical drop as small as real data shows. Accepted risk: Three reserves per player to calibrate, and the real late drop is mostly dead time, so true fatigue must stay small. Core.

    Long-term integration

    • Four guards stop long-save drift: each year’s generated youth cohort is anchored to a fixed target distribution by country and position; AI clubs give young players minutes by a real share and loan them when they cannot; AI clubs plan squads, replacing ageing stars and filling gaps each window; and club and league reputations drift toward a long-run value set by money and results, so no club stays big or small by inertia. Core.
    • The multi-season check bands against one pool of real history across leagues, because a save’s history diverges from real history anyway; the soft pull in AI choices is tuned to that pooled band. Core.
    • The multi-season check runs ten saves of fifty seasons each, so slow drift, such as generated-player attributes, reputation, and wages, shows as well as turnover; background leagues use the fast model. Accepted risk: Ten saves give few title-turnover samples per league, and fifty full seasons of the active leagues may not fit the overnight release run on the Mac mini and the VPS, so the size needs a cost check. Core.
    • The multi-season check bands three families: title turnover (distinct champions per decade, the longest title run, and how often a champion retains the title); fortune churn (season-to-season rank correlation, how often a top-four club drops out, and how far clubs rise and fall over ten seasons); and up and down rates (promoted clubs’ survival, relegated clubs’ return within one and two years, and yo-yo clubs). The champion’s points share is not banded. Core.
    • A player’s market value mostly follows long form and reputation over seasons, but a big tournament or a breakout half-season moves it quickly, as real hype does. Core.
    • A manager’s job security builds and falls over seasons: trophies, promotions, and seasons above expected build credit that fades slowly; success raises the board’s targets, so an over-performance becomes the next target; a new owner resets credit, targets, and patience; and AI clubs sack at real rates and moments, such as long losing runs, the relegation zone, and season end. Core.
    • Results move income four ways: real prize money per league place and cup round; attendance and season tickets that follow fan mood with a lag, so a bad season costs money the next season; commercial deals that renew at values set by reputation and recent success; and large European sums, which is one of the loops that entrenches the top and one of the loops the multi-season check watches. Core.
    • Every match keeps its weight four ways: the game shows the stakes before it, such as projected places, qualification and relegation chances, board and fan pressure, and prize money per place; mid-table clubs chase secondary goals, such as a cup run, a top-half finish, academy minutes, a record, or a rival’s result; matches with nothing at stake change players, as some switch off, some play for a contract or a place, and the manager can try players and tactics; and press, rivalry, and records give each match a story. Core.
    • A struggling club recovers four ways: it sells stars, cuts wages, and buys cheap young talent that grows; new owners take over at real rates, sometimes with bad results; luck evens out, and the game shows the manager the underlying numbers, such as expected goals, so an unlucky slump reads as unlucky; and a strong academy now and then produces a golden generation that lifts the club for some seasons. Core.
    • Balance comes first from real mechanisms, plus a gentle hidden pull toward the middle inside AI club decisions, such as transfer and wage choices, that the multi-season check tunes to a pooled real band. Accepted risk: A hidden pull can feel like rubber-banding if players discover it, so the pull must act only through AI choices a real club could make and never on match outcomes. Core.
    • Four forces limit a rich, winning club: each league’s real rules, such as revenue sharing, parachute payments, spending rules, and salary caps where they exist; wage pressure, as success raises contract demands and rival offers; ageing and poaching, as squads age together and bigger clubs buy the stars and the manager; and rivals who react, as AI clubs spend and change tactics to catch the leader and rival owners invest after failures. Core.
    • Mood stays without a runaway streak three ways: mood effects stay small and a season check bands streaks against real data, so a win streak is no more likely than chance; after a run of wins some players, by personality, relax and cut effort, while after losses some fight harder; and opponents raise effort and set up more cautiously against a leader or a team on a run. Core.
    • A player’s reputation moves four ways: ratings and minutes weighted by the level of the league and competition; big stages, such as finals, European nights, and tournaments, which count more; a bias toward visible goals and assists, as real scouts and media show, so creators and defenders are undervalued and good scouting finds an edge; and a slow fade without minutes or at a lower level. Core.
    • After each match the board reads four things, weighted by owner type: points against what the squad’s strength and budget predicted; the trend, so a run of bad results counts more than one loss and a recovery softens it; big matches, such as derbies, finals, and rivals; and style and academy minutes against the owner’s targets. Core.
    • Form runs at two speeds with a fading memory, so no match drops out suddenly: a short form of about three matches feeds mood and selection, and a long form of about a season feeds reputation and value. Core.
    • A young player’s growth has three layers: training sets the base rate by coaches, facilities, and personality; minutes at the right level of challenge add the most, fastest when the match level sits a little above the player’s ability; and good performances add a small extra. Growth moves within the player’s narrowing range of futures. Core.

    Fatigue, injuries, and fixture congestion

    • High-intensity running differs by role: wide midfielders about 3,140 metres a match, central midfielders 2,830, full-backs 2,610, attackers 2,340, centre-backs 1,830. Extra time runs about 10 percent below regulation for high-intensity running, speed, and distance, with accelerations and technique falling most. Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02640410802512775; https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1015-51632018000100016; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0110995.
    • Fear of re-injury is common after a hamstring injury and goes with both re-injury and lower performance; after a severe hamstring injury, top speed and high-speed running took up to five weeks of matches to recover, and a mild quadriceps injury about a week. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13223650/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884885/.
    • Treatment choices change time out: a partial meniscus removal returns in about 4 months against 6 for a repair, and a high ankle sprain 71 days after surgery against 39 without; surgery on some hamstring tears gives similar returns with fewer re-injuries. Days lost per injury type differ widely between clubs, lower where medical and performance staff communicate well and higher after head-staff turnover. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40815849/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9679836/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11877254/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10660205/.
    • Jet lag lasts about a day per time zone, worse eastward, and stayed raised four days after an 11-zone trip; away disadvantage grows with time zones crossed, but regular travel did not raise injuries. At about 43 degrees high-intensity running fell 26 percent while pass success rose; cooler northern clubs had more injuries and warmer southern clubs more ACL injuries; altitude favours the higher team. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8279034/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3378691/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22092416/.
    • An irregular match schedule, not total volume, goes with severe muscle injury across 30,097 player-seasons in the top five leagues, and load spikes carry 5 to 7 times the injury rate; compensatory training keeps non-starters’ fitness tests level but leaves them exposed to a spike when they play. Source: https://doi.org/10.1080/00913847.2026.2657258; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331267557; https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina57070645.
    • A full stop in the off-season costs 3 to 21 percent of aerobic capacity, against 1 to 4 percent for players who stay active, and two weeks off takes two to three weeks to rebuild. Teams average 30 preseason sessions (10 to 51); each 10 more go with 22 fewer days lost per 1000 hours in season and one point more match availability, a weak but real link. Training injuries spike in July. Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01407-4; https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546519899359; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12453838/.
    • Elite players fall ill about once every second season, with a median of 3 days out (58 percent respiratory, 38 percent stomach), peaking in winter; respiratory infections in the Bundesliga cost about 12 days each. No football data measures spread within a squad, so spread is a design choice. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27034126/ (73 teams, 1.26 million player-days); https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13084848/.
    • Premier League clubs used 30 to 34 players in 2023-24 and only five players played every minute; a title-winning Lille season had the lowest squad use and the fewest injuries. Football Manager players have complained of too many injuries in every version. Source: https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4025786; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25216043/; https://community.sports-interactive.com/forums/topic/594593-football-manager26-injuries/.
    • After an ACL injury, elite players return to matches after about 7.4 months, and only 59 to 65 percent play at the same level three years later, with lower minutes and output in the first two seasons back. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27034129/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29927499/.
    • The acute-to-chronic workload ratio has no evidence as a way to manage injury risk, and the ratio creates statistical artefacts, so load should not be modelled as that ratio. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/.
    • Recovery is not complete at 72 hours: hamstring force, physical performance (2 to 4 percent down), muscle damage markers, and soreness are still impaired, while hormonal and technical markers have recovered. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29098658/ (77 studies).
    • Congestion raises injury risk far more than it lowers running: one Champions League team saw 25.6 against 4.1 injuries per 1000 hours with two matches a week against one, with running unchanged; across 27 teams, 4 days’ rest or fewer raised muscle injuries by 32 percent; and the last 15 minutes of the second match in a two-match cycle carried 3.1 times the risk. Congestion barely changed results, and a meta-analysis found no effect on total distance. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20400751/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23851296/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26682867/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33068272/.
    • About 30 percent of injuries involve contact and 70 percent do not; foul play is involved in 40 percent of match ankle sprains; 61 percent of hamstring injuries happen while running or sprinting; and almost half of match hamstring injuries fall in the last 15 minutes of either half. Source: https://www.uefa.com/multimediafiles/download/uefaorg/medical/02/61/67/86/2616786_download.pdf; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23813486/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36588400/.
    • Re-injuries are 12 to 16 percent of injuries and keep players out longer; 18 percent of hamstring injuries are recurrences, 69 percent of those within two months of return. A previous injury is the strongest risk factor, about 2.7 times the risk of an injury the next season and 2 to 3 times for the same hamstring, groin, or knee injury. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7146935/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16855067/.
    • Median days out by diagnosis: contusion 4, functional muscle 4 to 5, concussion 5, groin 8, ankle sprain 8, hamstring, quadriceps, and calf strains 13, knee MCL 16, lateral meniscus 36, and ACL 205. Muscle injuries are about a third of all injuries (hamstring 37 percent of them, adductor 23, quadriceps 19, calf 13); severity runs about 17 percent minimal, 23 mild, 41 moderate, and 18 severe. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7146935/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21335353/.
    • Elite clubs see about 8 injuries per 1000 hours overall (about 21 to 27.5 in matches and 1.7 to 4.1 in training), about 2 injuries per player and 50 per squad each season, with about 86 percent of players available for a match; lower injury burden goes with more points. Incidence has fallen about 3 percent a season, while hamstring injuries rose from 12 to 24 percent of all injuries. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19553225/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23645832/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33547038/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36588400/.
    • Per minute of live play, high-speed running barely changes late in a match (plus 1.1 percent from minutes 0-30 to 60-90) while ball-in-play time falls 26.8 percent; forwards are the exception at minus 12.3 percent, and substitutes run 21.0 metres of high-speed running per live minute against 15.4 for starters. Source: .scratch/out/FINDINGS.md §16.2a (DFL, 7 matches, 57 full-match outfielders).

No band checks injury or fatigue rates. Source: crates/engine/src/player.rs:204; crates/engine/src/data/tuning.rs:129; content/realism-bands.json.
    • An injury today comes from two flat rolls scaled by injury resistance: the tackled player on a won tackle or foul, and every player once a minute. Fatigue does not change the chance, and an injury has no severity and no time out. Source: crates/engine/src/fatigue.rs:86; crates/engine/src/sim.rs:1009; content/tuning.json:83.

Below 0.7 one multiplier lowers speed, acceleration, passing, finishing, decisions, and composure together, and only regulation half-time gives energy back.     • Whether 50 or 100 matches is enough depends on how often the thing happens.     Contracts and the dressing room

    • In England the Premier League standard contract caps a fine at two weeks’ basic wage for a first offence and four weeks for later offences within twelve months, unless the players’ union approves more; a club can suspend a player on full pay for up to 14 days while it investigates, and the player appeals first to the club board and then to the League, with the sanction paused until the appeal ends. Source: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/579f1eef2e69cf81541f5565/t/57bc6605e58c62993057c155/1471964677468/standard+pl+contract.pdf and https://www.cnn.com/2011/10/28/sport/football/tevez-pfa-fine-manchester (research sub-agent).
    • Since 2018 FIFA’s transfer rules treat conduct meant to force a player to end or change a contract, such as training alone, as abusive, which gives the player just cause to leave with compensation; under a FIFA and FIFPRO agreement, the rules from 1 January 2027 expressly ban forcing a player to train in isolation or demoting a player as pressure. Source: https://www.mills-reeve.com/blogs/sports/april-2018/amendments-to-the-fifa-regulations-on-the-status-a/ and https://www.fifpro.org/en/articles/2026/06/what-the-fifpro-and-fifa-agreement-means-for-players (research sub-agent).
    • Discipline limits differ widely by country: Spain caps a fine at one twentieth of annual pay and bans keeping a player out of training except as a sanction or for injury; Italy caps a fine at 25 percent of monthly pay and allows training apart only as a sanction confirmed by an arbitration board; France bans fines outright and limits separate training groups; Portugal caps a fine at one third of monthly pay per offence; the Netherlands defaults to half a day’s wage per week. In Spain, Italy, and Portugal the usual remedy for abuse is ending the contract with compensation. Source: https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1985-12313 and https://www.assocalciatori.it/sites/default/files/attachment/pagina/Accordo collettivo AIC - Lega Serie A.pdf and https://sjogadores.pt/uploads/files/CCT Atualizado.pdf and https://www.vvcs.nl/documents/2023/10/CAO-Contractspelers-2023-2027-digitaal-exemplaar.pdf (research sub-agent).
    • Every source treats team selection as free: a club may leave a player out of matches without limit, and no rule limits public criticism as such, although dignity and harassment clauses in Italy, Portugal, and France come close. The fine cap for top-division German players is not public. Source: https://www.iberley.es/jurisprudencia/resolucion-contrato-trabajo-falta-ocupacion-efectiva-futbolista-profesional-sentencia-ts-sala-social-sec-1-rec-238-2008-28-04-2010-509771 and https://www.lto.de/recht/hintergruende/h/eren-derdiyok-hoffenheim-training-b-elf-beschaeftigungsanspruch-gleichbehandlung (research sub-agent).

    • The share of time the ball is above 1 metre differs between competitions, 12.4 percent in European elite matches against 19.4 percent in the A-League (measured on 20 SkillCorner A-League matches from 2024/25), so ball height is a measurable signal of playing identity rather than a constant. Source: .scratch/data/skillcorner (20 A-League matches 2024/25).
    • Club-to-club style varies far more than league-to-league: across 98 clubs in 1826 Wyscout matches from five leagues, pass accuracy spans 73 to 90 percent against 1.1 percentage points between leagues, pass volume varies 2.57 times, mean pass length spans 17.9 to 23.0 metres, and shot conversion spans 4.9 to 17.5 percent. Source: .scratch/out/wyscout_rates.json (1826 matches, five leagues).

Source: .scratch/out/idsse_players.csv (7 DFL matches, 247 full player-halves at 25 fps).

Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt:166 (the default rule only; the tactic effect is unverified).
    • Team strength has a measured size in 1826 Wyscout league matches: the best attack in a league scores about twice the league average and the worst about 0.6 times it, the home factor runs from 1.19 to 1.35, 71 to 84 percent of the spread in points per game is team quality, a top-quarter club beats a bottom-half club 71.0 percent of the time and loses 10.4 percent, and evenly matched games draw 4 to 6 points more often than two independent Poisson scorers predict. Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
    • Club style has two layers: one axis carries 59 percent of style variance and is mostly strength, with possession correlating 0.74 with points per game, while crosses and take-ons are almost independent of strength. Source: .scratch/out/gap_wy.txt (1826 Wyscout league matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.

    Tactics beyond Tier 2: phases, transitions, set pieces, live changes

    • The planned tactics model is Tier 2: a formation, a mentality, six team instructions, and a role and duty for each player, as written in the tactics-and-AI slice of the match-engine workflow. Source: .ai/workflows/football-manager-match-engine/03-slice-tactics-and-ai.md:41.
    • Real phase-of-play data uses nine paired phases rather than two: build-up, create, finish, direct, transition, quick break, set play, chaotic, and disruption, each locked one-to-one to the opponent’s high block, medium block, low block, and the matching defending phases. Measured on SkillCorner data from 20 A-League matches of 2024/25. Source: .scratch/data/skillcorner (20 A-League matches 2024/25).
    • The two phases differ by a measurable amount: in possession a team is 46 to 49 metres wide with a defensive line 34 to 37 metres up the pitch, and out of possession 36 to 38 metres wide with the line at 31 to 35 metres, while depth barely changes. Measured on Metrica tracking and event data from 2 matches at 25 frames per second. Source: .scratch/out/metrica2.json (2 matches, 25 fps tracking plus events).
    • The shape does not move uniformly between phases: on broadcast tracking from 20 matches (3.87 million samples), when a team wins the ball the back line advances about 3.5 metres while full-backs, wing-backs, and wingers advance 9 to 12 metres, so the team stretches from the back rather than sliding. Ground-truth tracking later halved these magnitudes and reversed the back line’s direction. Source: .scratch/out/sc_player_frames.csv.gz (20 matches, 3.87M samples).
    • The block tracks the ball at a fixed ratio, about 0.63 metres up the pitch for every metre the ball advances and 0.30 to 0.39 metres sideways for every metre it moves across, with the same slope in and out of possession; what changes between phases is width, 45.1 metres in possession against 35.4 out of it. Measured on SkillCorner broadcast data from 20 matches (1.76 million team-frames). Source: .scratch/out/sc_team_frames.csv.gz (20 matches, 1.76M team-frames).
    • Only 1.62 of the twenty outfielders stand within 5 metres of the ball and 3.71 within 10, the ten-metre zone is balanced at 1.84 attackers to 1.87 defenders, and 51.5 percent of live frames hold at most one player inside 5 metres, so the ball is uncontested more often than it is contested (2 Bundesliga matches, 154 thousand live frames). These figures were corrected after a position-code fix restored two wingers; the first version read 1.55, 3.55, 1.77 to 1.78, and 54.8 percent. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_crowd.csv (2 Bundesliga matches, 154k live frames).
    • Ground-truth tracking confirms the block law first measured on broadcast data: the team centroid moves 0.558 metres up the pitch per metre of ball advance in possession and 0.555 out of it, and 0.35 and 0.39 sideways, staying within 0.44 to 0.68 across 52 team-halves from 7 German league (DFL) matches, while width changes from 46.1 metres in possession to 36.8 out and depth changes by under 3 metres. Source: .scratch/out/idsse_shape.csv (52 team-halves, 7 DFL matches).
    • Ground-truth tracking corrects the broadcast finding that the back line advances about 3.5 metres and the wide players 9 to 12 metres when a team wins the ball. The ordering survives, with wide midfielders advancing 7.4 metres and full-backs 5.5 against a centre-back’s zero, but the magnitudes halve and the back line moves the other way: the deepest outfielder drops 1.4 metres deeper while the centroid advances 2.5, so the team stretches rather than sliding forward (52 team-halves, 7 DFL matches). Source: .scratch/out/idsse_shape.csv (52 team-halves, 7 DFL matches).
    • Both managers decide live: the AI manager changes its tactic during the match, the human can pause and change as well, and the human’s pre-set triggers for pressing, the defensive line, and similar situations are an extra tool rather than the only one. The code confirms the protocol already queues a tactics change or a substitution during a match (crates/protocol/src/command.rs); the AI side is not yet verified. Source: crates/protocol/src/command.rs:1 and crates/protocol/src/command.rs:11 (the change queue; the AI side is unverified).
    • Every live change, tactical or a substitution, takes effect at the next stoppage, for both managers; the plan already applies each queued change at a qualifying stoppage through the match-rules slice, so pausing gives the human time to think but no earlier effect. Source: crates/protocol/src/command.rs:2.
    • Goals come from both short and long possessions: possessions of 0 to 2 passes give 34 percent of goals and possessions of 10 or more give 25; a ball won in the final third leads to a shot 27.0 percent of the time against 9.7 in the own third; 34.1 percent of the 200 open-play losses a match are regained within 5 seconds; and a SkillCorner quick break leads to a shot 46 percent of the time. Measured on 200 StatsBomb matches and 20 SkillCorner matches. Source: .scratch/out/gap_sb.txt (200 StatsBomb matches) and FINDINGS.md §16; verified .scratch/out/gap_sc.txt (20 SkillCorner matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.
    • Pressing has measured triggers and the block a measured shape: a player receiving a forward pass is engaged 88.5 percent of the time against 59.5 after a backward pass, 78 percent when wide against 56 in the centre, and 82 percent against a low block; the defending block keeps a near-constant length of about 30 metres, its last defender advancing 0.49 metres and its midfield line 0.61 per metre of ball advance. Measured on 20 SkillCorner matches and 7 DFL matches with tracking joined to events. Source: .scratch/out/gap_sc.txt (20 SkillCorner matches) and FINDINGS.md §16; verified .scratch/out/gap_idsse_an.txt (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events) and FINDINGS.md §16.
    • Marking tightens near goal and hands over quickly: the nearest defending outfielder stands a median 2.4 metres from an attacker in the box-depth zone against 6.3 metres far from goal, and the nearest defender is still the same player 5 seconds later 64.0 percent of the time overall but only 51.3 percent near goal and 37.8 percent over 10 seconds. Measured on 7 DFL matches with tracking joined to events. Source: .scratch/out/gap_idsse_an.txt (7 DFL matches, tracking joined to events) and FINDINGS.md §16.
    • Set pieces are first-contact contests: a corner leads to a goal 3.45 percent of the time and to a shot 38.0 percent, the attacking team wins first contact on 37.2 percent of long corners and then shoots 68.9 percent of the time against 20.5 when the defenders win it, corners, free kicks, and penalties together give 36 percent of goals, and a direct free kick scores 3.2 percent of the time. Measured on 200 StatsBomb matches. Source: .scratch/out/gap_sb.txt (200 StatsBomb matches) and FINDINGS.md §16.

Source: .scratch/data/lastrow (19 goal sequences at 20 fps).

    What we are assuming

    Still open

    • FIFA’s calendar has international windows in September, October, November, March, and June; clubs must release players for them; and FIFA pays clubs for World Cup players and compensates clubs for injuries on duty. This is the agent’s recall, not yet checked.
    • Known real rivalries arrive only with an imported database, because the game ships no real data. A world with generated names gets its rivalries from geography and events in the save.
    • The crowd’s effect on players’ nerves and on late belief is a design value that must keep home advantage inside its measured range, because the public data measures the referee effect and the total but not the player effect.
    • Bonus-driven play and contract-year effort are design values small enough that the measured bands still hold, for example the shooting threshold at the edge of the box.
    • How much a divided squad plays worse is a design value, because no public match data measures dressing-room division. The value stays below the spread of skill between players.
    • A squad can run only a limited number of set-piece routines properly, and the limit is a design value, because no public data measures it.
    • The fast model can simulate several past seasons of every UEFA league inside a few minutes on the player’s machine.
    • A referee who leans towards the home side reads as realism rather than as the game cheating the player. Published research now measures that leaning (in fouls, cards, and added time) and ties it to the crowd, so the game copies a real effect rather than inventing one.
    • Flight arcs, heading duels, and spin on every ball update fit inside the 10 percent CPU tripwire the engine build records. Tracking data puts the ball above 1 metre for 12 to 19 percent of frames, so the aerial path is not a rare branch.
    • A realism budget measured against the shipped engine can be chosen before the additions are built, and the engine stays fast enough to run 1000 calibration matches inside it.
    • The engine can model the duel as a first-class contested action at roughly 456 a match without breaching its performance budget.
    • Each action family gets its own resolution model, with the action kind and the situation as the first inputs and the player’s attributes and mood as the second, so no single family carries player quality for the rest.
    • A hand-built choice model (the release mix by zone and pressure, a shooting threshold by distance, and a four-term receiver score with a pressure modifier) reproduces the measured shooting threshold at the edge of the box and the preference for the safe open receiver closely enough, without a learned model.
    • A generated backstory produces a history that holds together: a club’s past results, its partnerships, and its reputation agree with each other and with the squad on the books.
    • The cost of changing the event contract after the viewer, commentary, and report parts of the engine build are built is acceptable, and it is smaller than the cost of disturbing a complete part of the build today; every consumer of the event stream being still unbuilt today is the reason to doubt it.
    • Public data on real careers is enough to size age curves per attribute and the player-to-player variance around them, even though real data measures performance rather than attributes.
    • A real age curve measured on performance converts to an attribute curve through the same deliberate amplification the game applies to attribute effects (made larger than their measured sizes so that a management game rewards a better player), so a player’s decline reads on the pitch at the size real careers show.
    • Moments are one source of the player-to-player variance that real career data measures around the age curves, not an extra spread on top of it, so the curves as a whole still match real careers.
    • A result-only model can be calibrated to the full engine’s results (scorelines, player totals, injuries, and development inputs such as minutes and moments) closely enough that a simulated past reads as the same football as the present.
    • Training, coaching, and personality effects on development are set by design rather than by data, because no public data sizes them, and the career test bands only the factors the public research measures: playing time and level from the Transfermarkt career data, age peaks, physical curves, academy and loan outcomes, and ACL recovery.
    • The spread of loan outcomes can be set by design, because the Bundesliga loan study measures only the average effect of a loan and not the spread around it.
    • A valuation built from visible data only can be banded directly against the 650,000 Transfermarkt valuations in the public career dataset, because both read the same kind of visible inputs.
    • Owner types and financial rules together reproduce the measured shape of club losses (most clubs losing money and owners covering it within the rules) without a band that forces the share of loss-making clubs.
    • Real player and club names, likenesses, and badges can reach the game through a database the player imports, so the game itself ships no data that needs a licence.
    • The result-only model can run the leagues of all 55 UEFA member associations at once without making each matchday wait, because only the leagues the player chose run the full engine.
    • The team generator can produce believable competence ratings for every position without a scouting or history model behind them.
    • The two sources of disagreement about a player’s value, scouting noise and each club’s own weighting, can be calibrated together, so the spread of transfer fees bands cleanly even though one valuation can be wrong for two reasons at once.
    • Building the match-rules and tactics-and-ai slices against a player record that will change under them costs less than delaying both slices to change the record first.
    • Consistency can be an attribute that narrows a player’s match-to-match spread without adding team-level variance, so a player repeats while the team’s goal distribution stays Poisson.
    • The degree by which consistency narrows each action follows the order in which real players repeat (duels and physical output most, passing next, finishing and saving least), so the departure from the measured skill sizes is largest where the real data repeats most.
    • A weaker-foot rating can give two-footed players a visible edge while real rarity and a small effect size keep two-footedness from dominating squad choices.
    • A human can judge whether a simulated season reads as football from its table, its top scorers, and its transfer list, without watching any match inside it.
    • Goal-scoring in the engine can reproduce a Poisson distribution of total goals while mood, personality, and player quality all move the scoring rate.
    • Testing the physical bands (distance covered and time at each speed per outfield player, and the spread of peak sprint speed across a league) requires the engine to report per-player distance and speed, which the engine does not report today.
    • Ball height costs the engine little, because crates/engine/src/ball.rs already carries a third axis and a third velocity component, so the work is in the rules rather than in the state.
    • The realism test bands each action family against one named provider definition, StatsBomb for most on-ball and goalkeeper actions, SkillCorner for runs and pressing, and DFL tracking for carries, shielding, and goalkeeper positioning, rather than mixing definitions within a band.
    • The deliberate amplification of player effects can be confined to player-level outcomes, so the league bands (goals, home advantage, and per-match rates), the Poisson scoreline, and the club-to-club spread band still pass while only the per-player residual band fails.
    • One amplification size exists that is large enough for a manager to see a better player over about five matches and small enough for the best player’s season totals to stay inside the real range.
    • The realism test checks the style clash by fitting the measured possession rule (match possession equals 0.487 plus 0.949 times the difference in the two clubs’ season means, plus 0.027 at home) across every simulated match with AI managers on both sides, and the fitted multiplier lands near 0.949; the person gave no preference on the test, so this follows from the decision that the rule is an output of the matches and not an input, rather than from a choice.
    • Each StatsBomb definition has a DFL equivalent close enough to convert a band, and 7 DFL matches are enough to check the conversion even where they are too few to measure the band.
    • DFL tracking at 25 frames a second converts into a fixture of 50-tick frames by interpolation, with player identities mapped to shirt numbers, and the viewer shows it without a change to the viewer.
    • Adding more detail inside the planned model produces the emergent football that is missing when the numbers pass but the match still does not read like football.
    • A manager-set trigger can live in the rule pack and the change queue, so no conditional tactic logic enters the engine’s tick loop.
    • Nine paired phases can be derived from the engine’s own state rather than authored by the manager, so the tactic interface stays at two phases while the model runs nine.
    • A team’s block can be made to track the ball at the fixed measured ratio (about 0.63 metres up the pitch and 0.30 to 0.39 metres sideways per metre the ball moves) without explicit per-player marking logic in the tick loop.
    • The AI manager’s live decisions enter the match through the same change queue as the human’s, so both sides change tactics the same way and no tactic logic runs inside the tick loop; this extends the assumption about manager-set triggers to every live change.
    • A per-player transition default can be derived from the team’s two transition choices and the player’s role and attributes, so a manager who never opens the per-player screen still gets a coherent team transition.
    • The engine can keep club identity, squad, and tactic as separate inputs to style, so the realism test can check a club-to-club spread band and still tell which layer produced the spread.
    • AI managers that pick tactics from their four inputs (philosophy, opponent, match state, and season situation) produce, over a season, choices whose results reproduce the measured possession rule with its 0.949 multiplier, so the rule emerges as an output without being tuned directly.
    • The three effects of the tactical attribute (information accuracy, learning speed for a new tactic, and the squad’s mood and trust response) apply to AI managers as well as to the human, so both sides of a match are modelled the same way and only the source of the choices differs.
    • A player of the game can tell mood-driven errors from an ignored instruction, because the squad’s mood and trust are visible to the manager before and during the match.
    • The other matches of a matchday fit in the background of a real-time match on the player’s machine without slowing the player’s own match or its view.
    • A player of the game reads a habit that overrides an instruction under pressure as realism, because the match view or the report names the habit and the moment, and not as the game ignoring the manager. This is, in a narrower form, the same risk as the rejected bet that players would accept instructions landing weaker under a weak manager.

    Confirmed

    • All four costs would hurt a player: waiting on matchdays, save creation time, save size, and memory. Confirmed.
    • Team numbers today have three problems: nothing shows what they change, familiarity and partnerships may be counted twice (in each player and again for the team), and club identity is a word rather than a number the engine reads. Confirmed.
    • The realism bands measured on StatsBomb open data can still calibrate a commercial game, because calibration uses derived statistics rather than shipping the data; StatsBomb’s research framing makes this a question for StatsBomb, not a settled right. You confirmed it: you accept the risk.
    • Whether a manager may keep a hurt player on the pitch is still unanswered; giving injuries a severity and a risk driven by play settles severity and risk, but not the manager’s choice.
    • Personality fits the existing 1-to-100 attribute scale and needs no new data type.
    • A milestone review that a person performs by hand catches what the shape measures miss, and it happens early enough to catch a failed mechanism before the rest of the model is built on it.
    • Real football publishes a distribution of scorelines and a team-to-team spread that the test can band against, the same way it published the three averages in the planned realism bands.
    • The plans already in progress are the floor, so every idea on this board adds scope and none removes planned scope.
    • The charter of the football-manager-match-engine workflow still describes that workflow correctly, although the product is now read as a management game and the charter records exclusions (leagues, seasons, competitions, transfers, and finances) that the product no longer honours.
    • The manager is a modelled entity with attributes, and not only the AI module the shape document already names.

    Rejected

    • The match engine ships first and the season layer builds on it, so no season requirement changes the engine’s data contract after the fact. Rejected, because the decision to add the player identifier to the event contract when the season layer starts changes that contract after the fact on purpose.
    • Per-player match ratings can be derived from the event stream the engine already emits. Rejected by a read of the code at the time, which found no player identifier on the match event.
    • The realism programme still fits the earlier decision that every idea on this board belongs to one second workflow, separate from the match-engine build, although the full season layer (table, availability, calendar, and cups) makes that workflow larger than the match engine it follows. Rejected when the work was split into two programmes, engine realism and the season layer.
    • One past season is enough history for a partnership to read as earned, so a pair with one season of shared minutes is distinguishable from a pair with none. Rejected when the save moved to several simulated past seasons.
    • A transfer market and finances can be built on the attributes that the work to deepen the player model supplies, with no scouting model, no contract-negotiation model, and no separate player-value model.
    • A duel resolved from attributes, mood, and ball state reproduces the real pass-completion curve by kind of pass (from 91.7 percent for a simple pass down to 30.7 percent for a cross) as an emergent result, so no probability table for each kind of pass is written by hand.
    • Attribute effects sized to the spreads measured in real football, about plus or minus 7 points of pass completion and 5 of shot conversion between the best and worst players, still read to a manager as real differences between players.
    • The three aggregate calibration bands (goals, shots, and possession) are a sufficient test of realism.
    • The three calibration bands still hold once mood and personality move decisions; the extra variance does not push goals, shots, or possession outside them.
